### Question 1 (9 marks)

**(a)** [3 marks]

- An ecosystem is a self-sustaining unit in which living things and their non-living surroundings interact.
- It is a community of living things together with the soil, water, air and sunlight around them.
- It is any natural unit, such as a pond or a forest, in which the living and non-living parts interact.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). Accept 'a place where living things and their surroundings interact'. Do NOT accept 'a habitat' alone - a habitat is the home of ONE kind of organism, while an ecosystem is the whole interacting unit.*

**(b)** [2 marks]

- A pond
- A forest
- A river or a stream
- A lake (e.g. the Volta Lake)
- A mangrove swamp
- A grassland or savanna
- A farm or a school garden
- The branches of a large tree
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any TWO correct examples (2 marks). Accept any real ecosystem found in Ghana. Do NOT accept 'a zoo' or 'a garden in a pot' without the surroundings.*

**(c)** [4 marks]

- The living parts supply what the other living parts need: plants make the food, animals spread pollen and seeds, and decay organisms return nutrients to the soil.
- The non-living parts - sunlight, water, air and soil - provide what the living parts need.
- Green plants trap the Sun's energy and make food, so the ecosystem has its own source of energy.
- Waste and dead remains are broken down and used again, so the nutrients go round and round instead of being lost.
- Because of these links the unit can keep itself going without people having to supply anything from outside.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any FOUR correct points (4 marks). The key ideas are: the parts supply each other's needs, energy enters through green plants, and nutrients are recycled. Award at least 1 mark for 'the parts depend on each other so it can keep going'.*

  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). The key idea is that an arboreal ecosystem is found IN TREES. Award at least 1 mark for 'it is found in trees'.*

### Question 4 (9 marks)

**(a)** [3 marks]

- One living thing feeds on another (feeding), e.g. a goat eating grass.
- Animals carry pollen from flower to flower, so the plants can make seeds.
- Animals carry seeds to new places and spread them in their droppings.
- Animals provide shelter for one another, e.g. birds nesting in trees.
- Decay organisms break down dead plants and animals and return nutrients to the soil.
- Large animals trample or browse plants and so change what grows where.
- Living things compete with one another for food, water, light and space.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). Each point must say how one LIVING thing affects another. Do NOT accept 'the rain waters the plants' here - that is abiotic affecting biotic.*

**(b)** [3 marks]

- Sunlight allows the water plants to make their food, and warms the water.
- The temperature of the water affects how much oxygen dissolves in it and how fast the organisms grow.
- Oxygen dissolved in the water is what the fish and other water animals breathe.
- The water itself is the home and the support of the organisms in the pond.
- Mineral salts in the water and in the mud feed the water plants.
- In the dry season the water level falls, so the organisms are crowded into less space and there is less oxygen.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). The answer must link a named ABIOTIC component to its effect on a living thing.*

**(c)** [3 marks]

- Plants take in carbon dioxide and give out oxygen, so they change the air around them.
- Plants and animals add humus to the soil when they die and decay.
- Tree roots break up rocks, hold the soil together and stop it being washed away.
- Plants slow down the wind and shade the ground, so the soil stays cooler and moister.
- Plants take water from the soil and give it out through their leaves, which adds moisture to the air.
- Animals tread on the soil and their droppings add nutrients to it.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). The answer must show a LIVING thing changing a NON-LIVING part. Award at least 1 mark for an example such as 'plants give out oxygen' or 'roots hold the soil'.*

### Question 5 (8 marks)

**(a)** [3 marks]

- Interdependence means that the organisms in an ecosystem depend on one another to stay alive.
- Each organism needs something that another organism provides: food, shelter, pollination or seed dispersal.
- It also means that what happens to one organism affects the others.
- Because of interdependence, the removal of one kind of organism can harm many others.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). The key word is DEPEND. Accept 'they rely on each other'.*

**(b)** [2 marks]

- Animals depend on plants for food and for the oxygen they breathe.
- Plants depend on animals for the carbon dioxide they use to make food.
- Many plants depend on insects and other animals to carry pollen so they can make seeds.
- Many plants depend on animals to carry their seeds to new places.
- Animals depend on plants for shelter and for places to build nests.
- Plants depend on decay organisms, helped by animals, to return nutrients to the soil.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any TWO correct points (2 marks). Each point must show dependence in BOTH directions, or at least be clearly about plants and animals depending on each other.*

**(c)** [3 marks]

- Any sensible Ghanaian food chain, e.g. maize -> grasshopper -> lizard -> hawk; or grass -> goat -> human; or leaves -> caterpillar -> hen -> human.
- The chain must start with a green plant (the producer) and the arrows must point in the direction the food passes.
- If one link is removed, the organisms that feed on it lose their food and their numbers fall.
- The organisms that the removed link used to feed on may increase in number because nothing is eating them.
- So the balance of the whole ecosystem is disturbed, not just one link.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark for a correct food chain and 1 mark each for any TWO correct effects of removing a link (3 marks). The chain must begin with a plant. Award at least 1 mark for 'the animals that eat it lose their food'.*

**(a)** [3 marks]

- The animals that feed on the insects, such as lizards, birds and frogs, lose their food and may move away or become fewer.
- The flowers that needed the insects for pollination are not pollinated, so they produce fewer seeds and fruits.
- With fewer insects to eat the pests, the pest insects may come back in larger numbers later.
- The maize may at first look better, but the yield may fall because there is less pollination.
- The insecticide may also wash into the soil and the water and harm other organisms.
- The birds and other animals that eat the poisoned insects may be poisoned too.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). Accept any sensible consequence. Do NOT accept 'the maize grows better' on its own.*

**(b)** [3 marks]

- Because the organisms in an ecosystem are interdependent: each one depends on the others for food, pollination and other needs.
- So whatever happens to the insects passes along the food chain to the animals that eat them, and then to the animals that eat those.
- It also affects the plants, because many of them need insects for pollination.
- Nothing in an ecosystem lives on its own, so no effect stops with the organism it started with.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). The key word is INTERDEPENDENCE, or the idea of a chain reaction along the food chain. Award at least 1 mark for 'everything depends on everything else'.*

**(c)** [2 marks]

- Plant different crops together or in rotation, so that no one pest builds up.
- Use a mild, local method such as neem extract or wood ash instead of a strong chemical.
- Leave a strip of the farm uncultivated so that useful insects and birds have somewhere to live.
- Spray only in the evening, when the bees and other useful insects are not flying.
- Spray only when it is necessary, and only the part of the farm that is affected.
- Hand-pick the pests, or encourage birds and lizards that eat them.
- Add compost or manure so that the crops are strong enough to resist attack.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any TWO sensible suggestions (2 marks). Award method marks: any suggestion that would really reduce the harm earns credit. Do NOT accept 'stop farming'.*

### Question 7 (7 marks)

**(a)** [4 marks]

- Choose one ecosystem near the school, e.g. a pond, a stream, a patch of bush or one large tree.
- Visit it more than once, at the same time of day, and observe quietly.
- Note the ABIOTIC components: the soil, the water, the temperature, the light, the rainfall.
- List the BIOTIC components: the plants, the animals and any decay organisms seen.
- Record where each organism was found and, where possible, what it was doing.
- Draw or photograph the ecosystem and label what is in it.
- Record the information in a table with two columns, biotic and abiotic.
- Note any sign of interaction, e.g. a bee on a flower, a caterpillar eating a leaf, a bird in a branch.
- Work in groups, compare notes afterwards and write a short report.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any FOUR correct points (4 marks). This is a design task: award method marks for any sensible step. The three things to look for first are: choosing and visiting a real ecosystem, recording both biotic and abiotic components, and noting interactions.*

**(b)** [3 marks]

- It shows the class that the living and non-living parts really do interact, not just in a book.
- It makes the class notice how the organisms depend on one another.
- It helps the class to see what happens when people damage an ecosystem.
- Working together on it helps everyone in the group to join in.
- To avoid harm: do not pick the flowers or break the branches.
- To avoid harm: put back any stone, log or leaf that is turned over.
- To avoid harm: do not take animals home and do not leave litter behind.
- To avoid harm: keep to the paths and do not trample the plants or pollute the water.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any TWO sensible reasons and 1 mark for a sensible precaution (3 marks). Accept any reason that shows the study added something a book cannot. Do NOT accept 'it is fun' on its own.*

### EXAMINER NOTES

- This sub-strand has ONE indicator with FIVE exemplars, so the blueprint shows the same indicator code throughout and varies only the exemplar number. That is correct and expected here - do not read it as a mapping error.
- Question 3 is exemplar 3, 'identify and list the components'. The six blanks are numbered (i) to (vi), so marking is quick and fair: 1 mark each, and the blank number must be matched to the answer. Accept 'living' for biotic and 'non-living' for abiotic.
- Questions 4 and 6 are exemplar 5, 'explain how the components affect one another'. The hardest part for pupils is the DIRECTION of the effect. Question 4(a) wants living-on-living, 4(b) non-living-on-living, and 4(c) living-on-non-living - mark each against its own direction and do not accept an answer given in the wrong one.
- Question 5(c) expects a food chain, not a food web. The chain must start with a green plant and the arrows must point the way the food passes. Do NOT expect the words producer, consumer, trophic level or a pyramid of numbers - all of that is beyond B7.
- Question 6 is the insecticide scenario. It is the best single test of interdependence in the paper: many pupils will answer 'the maize grows better', which is exactly the misconception the question is written to expose.
- Topic 9.5 carries 24 of 100 marks, the highest weighting, because interdependence is named in the content standard itself, not only in the exemplars. If you want a flatter spread, move marks from Questions 5 and 6 to Questions 1 and 2.
