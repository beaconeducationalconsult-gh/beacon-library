### Question 5 (9 marks)

**(a)** [4 marks]

- Always cut away from the body.
- Carry tools with the blade or sharp edge pointing downwards.
- Use each tool only for the job it was made for.
- Put a tool that is not being used down safely, away from the working area.
- Wear protective clothing such as boots and gloves.
- Never leave tools lying on a path or in the grass where someone may step on them.
- Repair or report any damaged tool before using it again.
- Children should use tools only under the supervision of an adult.
- Read the label before using any chemical, and wash afterwards.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any FOUR correct rules (4 marks). Do NOT accept a rule that belongs to maintenance rather than handling, such as 'oil the blade after use'.*

**(b)** [3 marks]

- A blunt cutlass does not bite into the material easily, so the farmer has to use much more force.
- The extra force makes the blade harder to control, so it is more likely to slip.
- When it slips it can cut the farmer's leg, hand or foot.
- A sharp cutlass cuts with a light, controlled stroke, so it goes where the farmer wants it to go.
- Rushing and forcing a blunt blade is what causes most cutlass accidents.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). The key chain is: blunt needs more force, more force means less control, less control means the blade slips. Award at most 1 mark for an answer that only says 'it can cut you'.*

  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any TWO correct points (2 marks). Accept any sensible safety step immediately after spraying.*

**(b)** [3 marks]

- Wash or wipe off all the soil and moisture from the blade.
- Dry the hoe thoroughly in the sun or with a cloth.
- Sharpen the blade if it has become blunt.
- Oil or grease the metal part lightly to keep rust away.

### Question 7 (9 marks)

**(a)** [3 marks]

- Farmers clean, dry and oil shared tools after use.
- Tools are kept in a dry, locked community store.
- The community sets rules about who may use the tools and how they are cared for.
- Farmers meet to repair or replace damaged tools together.
- Pupils and farmers assemble tools from the community and practise using and maintaining them.
- A visit is made to a community farm to observe and report on how tools are handled.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). Accept any sensible way in which a community looks after its tools.*

**(b)** [4 marks]

- Ask permission from the farm owner and agree a date and time with the class.
- Decide beforehand what the class is looking for, and prepare a short list or checklist.
- Move round the farm in a group, watching how each tool is carried, used and put down.
- Look at how the tools are cleaned, dried, sharpened and stored after work.
- Ask the farmer or workers questions about the rules they follow.
- Write down or draw each tool seen, with a note of its condition and how it is cared for.
- Keep clear of the working area so that the class does not cause an accident.

**(c)** [2 marks]

- The names of the tools seen on the farm.
- What each tool is used for.
- How each tool was handled.
- How each tool was maintained.

### EXAMINER NOTES

- Question 5(b) is the discriminator, and it is the one most pupils get backwards. They assume a sharp cutlass is the dangerous one. It is not: a blunt blade will not bite, so the farmer forces it, loses control, and the blade slips onto a leg or a hand. The note withholds marks from an answer that only says 'it can cut you' - the reasoning is what earns them.
- Question 7(b) is the design task, and it is marked on two halves. Pupils must say something about PREPARING for the visit and something about what they would OBSERVE AND RECORD. An answer that covers only one half is capped at 3 of the 4 marks. Do not insist on a particular farm - the school farm, a nearby cocoa or maize farm, or a community animal farm all work.
- Question 1(b) asks for four tools OTHER than the one in Fig. 1. Several pupils will write 'hoe' because it is the first tool they think of, and that mark is withheld. Worth saying out loud before the mock.
- Question 6(a) is marked on the match, not on the rules alone. A pupil who lists four correct safety rules but attaches them to the wrong tools scores nothing. Tell them to write the tool and the rule together in one sentence.
- The two indicators under this content standard are 'explain the basic rules' (B7/JHS1.4.5.1.1) and 'apply the handling and maintenance in their community' (B7/JHS1.4.5.1.2). That is why the paper moves from naming tools, through meanings and rules, to a community visit and a report - the curriculum's own sequence, not mine.
- Topic weighting is 30 marks for 15.1, 25 for 15.2, 27 for 15.3 and 18 for 15.4. Tools and safety carry the most. If you spent longer on community work, move marks towards Question 7.
- Two cheap practicals that raise marks sharply: (1) bring a hoe, a cutlass and a rake to class and let pupils name the parts and pass them round safely, and (2) leave two nails, one oiled and one wet, overnight and look at them the next morning. The first teaches Questions 1 and 6, the second teaches Questions 3 and 4.
- If you can arrange it, the farm visit in Question 7(b) is worth doing for real. A single afternoon at a nearby farm fixes the whole sub-strand better than any number of notes, and it gives pupils something concrete to write about.
