## ANSWER KEY AND MARKING SCHEME

**2.1  2.1 What is a Cell? Introduction to Living Cells**

- **Q1** (3 marks) A single cell can only contract with the force of one cell. A tissue is many similar cells of the same kind working together, so their combined force is far greater and is enough to move a part of the body. One cell on its own cannot produce enough force to do that work.
- **Q2** (3 marks) It is an organism, not a tissue. The diagram shows that a tissue is a group of similar cells working together as part of a larger living thing. An amoeba is a single cell that carries out all of the life processes by itself, so it is a whole organism.

**2.2  2.2 Structure and Functions of the Animal Cell**

- **Q3** (3 marks) The cell membrane is the outer boundary: it holds the cell together and CONTROLS which substances enter and leave. The cytoplasm is the jelly-like material inside: it fills the cell and is where the chemical reactions of the cell take place.
- **Q4** (4 marks) The cell would run out of usable energy and would eventually die. Mitochondria carry out respiration, releasing the energy in food in a form the cell can use. Without that energy the cell cannot carry out the processes - growth, repair, moving substances - that keep it alive.

**2.3  2.3 Organelles of the Animal Cell and Their Functions**

- **Q5** (3 marks) 2 - mitochondrion: carries out respiration to release energy from food. 5 - vacuole: stores water, food and waste. (Also accept 5 - cytoplasm, which is where the cell's chemical reactions happen, depending on the reading of the figure.)
- **Q6** (3 marks) The nucleus contains the genetic material, which carries the instructions for making the cell's proteins and for everything the cell does. Because those instructions determine what the cell is and what it makes, the nucleus controls all the cell's activities.

**2.4  2.4 Structure and Functions of the Plant Cell**

- **Q7** (4 marks) A plant has no skeleton, so each of its cells must support itself. The cell wall is made of cellulose and is rigid, so it holds the cell firm and lets the plant stand upright. An animal has a skeleton to support its body, so its cells do not need a wall - and a wall would in fact stop them working, because animal cells such as muscle cells must be able to change shape.
- **Q8** (3 marks) The vacuole stores water and dissolved substances, and the water inside pushes the cytoplasm out against the wall so that the cell stays firm and the plant does not wilt. An animal cell does not rely on this for support because the animal has a skeleton, so its vacuoles are small and are used only for storage and for moving substances about inside the cell.

**2.5  2.5 Organelles of the Plant Cell and Their Functions**

- **Q9** (3 marks) 1 - cell wall.  4 - large central vacuole.  5 - chloroplast.
- **Q10** (3 marks) Chloroplasts contain chlorophyll, which traps light energy for photosynthesis. A plant in the dark has no light to trap, so it makes few chloroplasts because they would be of no use. This shows that the chloroplast is the organelle where the plant makes its own food using light.

**2.6  2.6 Differences and Similarities Between Plant and Animal Cells**

- **Q11** (3 marks) Any three: the plant cell has a cell wall, the animal cell does not. The plant cell has chloroplasts, the animal cell does not. The plant cell has one large central vacuole, the animal cell has small vacuoles. The plant cell has a regular, fixed rectangular shape, the animal cell has a rounded, irregular shape.
- **Q12** (4 marks) The two parts do different jobs. The cell membrane controls which substances enter and leave the cell, and every cell needs this. The plant also needs the rigid cell wall outside the membrane to hold the cell firm and support the plant, because a plant has no skeleton. The wall cannot replace the membrane because it is fully permeable - it lets everything through, so it cannot control what enters or leaves.
- **Q13** (3 marks) The reasoning is wrong because BOTH plant and animal cells have a nucleus, so a nucleus does not identify which kind of cell it is. The pupil should look for a cell wall, for chloroplasts, or for one large central vacuole, and at whether the cell has a fixed rectangular shape - these are found only in plant cells.

**PRACTICAL TASK**

- **Q14** (3 marks) Any two with a correction: the mirror or light source is not angled at the hole in the stage - turn it so that light is reflected up through the specimen. The objective lens is not lined up with the hole in the stage - rotate the nosepiece until it clicks into place. The slide is not sitting over the hole - move the slide across. The diaphragm is closed - open it.
- **Q15** (2 marks) A light microscope forms its image using light that passes THROUGH the specimen. If the specimen is too thick, light cannot get through it and it looks dark, so nothing can be seen.
- **Q16** (3 marks) Watch the objective from the SIDE while using the coarse knob to bring it down close to the slide. Then look down the eyepiece and use the coarse knob to focus by moving the lens UP, away from the slide. Watching from the side lets you see how close the lens is and stop before it touches the slide; focusing upwards means you can never push the lens into the glass.

## EXAMINER NOTES

- Pitched at the compulsory diagram question of BECE Paper 2: 72% of the marks are at Apply level or above and 36% are Challenging. Use it to stretch pupils who already know the labels.
- Q7 and Q12 are the two hardest and they test the same idea from different directions: WHY a plant needs a wall. Pupils can name the parts but cannot say why the plant needs one and the animal does not. The answer they must reach is support - a plant has no skeleton, so every cell must hold itself up.
- Q12 has a second layer most pupils miss: the wall cannot do the membrane's job because it is fully permeable. If a pupil says 'the wall protects the cell and the membrane controls what goes in and out', they have both halves and deserve full marks.
- Q13 is the classic reasoning error - 'it has a nucleus so it must be a plant cell'. Both cells have a nucleus. Pupils should be taught to look for the wall, the chloroplasts or the single large vacuole instead.
- Q4, Q10 and Q14 all ask pupils to PREDICT or SUGGEST rather than recall. These are the words BECE uses and the ones pupils handle worst, because there is nothing to remember - the answer has to be reasoned from the diagram or from what the organelle does.
- The microscope in Fig. 7 is the cheapest practical in the syllabus and the one most likely to appear. If your school has even one working microscope, twenty minutes with an onion skin turns Q14 to Q16 into near-free marks. If it does not, the questions still work from the diagram alone.
- Q16 is worth teaching as a routine rather than an answer: focus DOWN while watching from the side, then focus UP while looking through the eyepiece. Say it as a rhyme and pupils remember it, and it saves the slides and the objective lenses.
