## ANSWER KEY AND MARKING SCHEME

**8.1  8.1 What is the Solar System?**

- **Q1** (4 marks) The sun is at the centre. The sun's gravity pulls on every planet, and that inward pull is what holds each planet in its orbit. Without it, a planet would carry on in a straight line and travel off into space. Each planet is moving forwards, and the sun's pull bends that forward motion into a curve, so the planet goes round the sun instead of either falling into it or flying away.
- **Q2** (3 marks) Any one with an explanation. The inner four are small and rocky while the outer four are much bigger - the diagram shows the outer orbits are far apart and the planets drawn on them are larger. Or: the outer planets take far longer to go once round the sun, because their orbits are much larger so they have a much greater distance to travel. Or: the inner planets are hot and the outer ones cold, because the inner four are close to the sun.

**8.2  8.2 The Galaxy and the Milky Way**

- **Q3** (3 marks) The Milky Way is a galaxy - an enormous collection of many millions of stars held together by gravity. Our sun is ONE of those stars, and it sits out in one of the spiral arms, a long way from the centre of the galaxy, not at the centre of it.
- **Q4** (3 marks) They are completely different sizes of thing. The solar system is ONE star, the sun, together with the planets, moons and other bodies going round it. The Milky Way is a galaxy containing many millions of stars, and the sun is only one of them. So the solar system sits inside the Milky Way as a tiny part of it - they cannot be the same thing.

**8.3  8.3 The Inner Planets: Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars**

- **Q5** (3 marks) Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars. Mercury is nearest the sun.
- **Q6** (4 marks) A year is the time a planet takes to travel once round the sun. Mercury's orbit is much smaller than the earth's because Mercury is much closer to the sun, so it has a far shorter distance to cover. Mercury also travels faster along its orbit than the earth does. Smaller distance and greater speed both mean one complete journey round the sun takes far less time - 88 earth days against the earth's 365.

**8.4  8.4 Properties of the Inner Planets**

- **Q7** (4 marks) The year gets longer as you move outwards: 88 earth days on Mercury, 225 on Venus, 365 on Earth, 687 on Mars. The reason is that the further a planet is from the sun, the larger its orbit, so it has a greater distance to travel to get once round. The further planets also move more slowly along their orbits, so the journey takes longer still.
- **Q8** (3 marks) A day and a year are two completely separate movements, and their lengths do not have to be related. A day is the time the planet takes to spin once on its own axis; a year is the time it takes to travel once round the sun. Venus spins on its axis extremely slowly - more slowly than it goes round the sun - so one spin takes 243 earth days while one orbit takes only 225.

**8.5  8.5 Movement of Mercury and Venus Around the Sun**

- **Q9** (4 marks) Both orbits lie INSIDE the orbit of the earth, so Mercury and Venus are always in roughly the same direction from the earth as the sun is. We can therefore only ever see them near the sun in the sky - low in the west just after sunset, or low in the east just before sunrise. At midnight the sun, and with it the direction these planets lie in, is on the far side of the earth, so they cannot be seen then.
- **Q10** (3 marks) Mercury takes less time. The diagram shows Mercury's orbit is the smaller of the two, so Mercury is closer to the sun and has a shorter distance to travel to complete one orbit. A smaller orbit means a shorter journey, and so a shorter year.

**8.6  8.6 Elliptical Paths of the Planets**

- **Q11** (3 marks) An ellipse is an oval shape - like a circle that has been stretched out in one direction. In the orbit shown the sun is not at the centre of the ellipse but at one FOCUS, which is one of the two points set inwards from the ends of the oval.
- **Q12** (3 marks) Because the orbit is an ellipse and not a circle, and the sun sits at one focus rather than at the centre. That means one end of the orbit is nearer the sun than the other. The diagram shows this: the distance marked NEAREST is shorter than the distance marked FURTHEST, so the earth's distance from the sun changes as it goes round.

**PRACTICAL TASK**

- **Q13** (4 marks) Push a stick upright into flat, open ground. Mark the position of the tip of its shadow on the ground every hour, and note the time beside each mark. Through the day the shadow swings round and changes length - long in the morning, shortest at midday, long again in the evening, pointing the other way by late afternoon. The sun is not moving round the earth, so the only way the shadow can swing round like that is if the earth itself is turning. The pattern of marks is the record of the earth's rotation.
- **Q14** (3 marks) At midday the sun is higher in the sky than at any other time of the day - it is more nearly overhead. The higher the sun is, the more steeply its rays come down, and the more steeply they come down the shorter the shadow they cast. So the shortest shadow marks the moment the sun is highest.
- **Q15** (3 marks) Any one with a remedy. The ground may not be level, which tilts the stick and falsely changes the shadow length - check with a spirit level or choose the flattest ground you can find. The tip of the shadow is fuzzy and hard to mark exactly - mark the middle of the fuzzy edge, and have the same person judge it every time. The stick may lean over during the day - check it stays upright. Cloud may hide the sun for part of the day - choose a clear day, or repeat on another day and compare.

## EXAMINER NOTES

- Pitched at the compulsory diagram question of BECE Paper 2: 70% of the marks are at Apply level or above and 38% are Challenging.
- Q1 is the one pupils answer with 'gravity' and nothing else, and they score one of four. To get the rest they must say what the pull DOES: it bends the planet's forward motion into a curve, so the planet neither falls into the sun nor flies off in a straight line. Teach it as 'forwards plus inwards equals around'.
- Q4 on the Milky Way against the solar system is the confusion I meet most. Pupils use the two words interchangeably. The scale contrast is the cure: the solar system has one star, the galaxy has millions, and the sun is one of them.
- Q8 - a day on Venus longer than its year - is the best 'explain how this can be true' question in the paper, because the answer is that the two movements are independent. Pupils assume a year must be longer than a day and are stuck. Venus spins so slowly that it breaks the assumption.
- Q9, on why Mercury and Venus are never seen at midnight, is genuinely hard and needs the diagram. Their orbits are inside ours, so their direction from us is always close to the sun's direction. Very few pupils reach this without seeing Fig. 5.
- The shadow-stick practical in Fig. 7 is the best astronomy practical available to a school with no equipment at all - a stick, some chalk and a level playground. Run it across a whole school day with hourly marks and Q13 to Q15 become straightforward. It also gives pupils a real record of rotation rather than a diagram they have to believe.
- Note on Q11: 'oval' is worth one mark, but the point pupils miss is the FOCUS - the sun is off-centre, which is exactly why the distance changes in Q12. The two questions are a pair; teach them together.
