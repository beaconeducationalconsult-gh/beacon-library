- Some of the energy put into the machine is always changed into forms that are not useful, mainly heat and sound, because of friction between the moving parts.
- That wasted energy is not destroyed - it has gone into the surroundings - but it is no longer doing the job the machine was built for, so the useful energy out is always less than the energy put in.

*Marking note: Up to 2 marks: 1 for saying some energy is wasted, and 1 for naming friction, heat or sound as the cause. Do NOT accept 'because the machine is old'.*

### Question 17  [12 marks]

**(a)**

- Kwashiorkor - protein.
- Night blindness - vitamin A.
- Rickets - vitamin D, calcium or phosphorus.
- Scurvy - vitamin C.
- Anaemia - iron.
- Goitre - iodine.

*Marking note: Up to 6 marks: up to 2 for each of THREE diseases, 1 for the disease and 1 for the missing nutrient. Give 0 if the nutrient is paired with the wrong disease.*

**(b)**

- Eat a balanced diet with foods from all the food groups, especially beans, fish, eggs, green vegetables and fruit.
- Grow a kitchen garden or keep a few animals so that the family has its own supply of vegetables, eggs and milk.
- Use fortified foods, such as iodised salt, vitamin A-fortified oil or iron-fortified flour.
- Teach mothers and pupils about the causes of deficiency diseases at the clinic and at school.
- Take children for regular weighing and check-ups at the clinic so that a problem is caught early.

*Marking note: Up to 4 marks: up to 2 for each of TWO ways, 1 for the way and 1 for how it helps. Give 1 for a bare 'eat good food'. Do NOT accept 'take medicine' on its own.*

**(c)**

- Antibiotics kill bacteria. They have no effect on viruses, because viruses are not bacteria and are not alive in the same way; they grow inside the body's own cells.
- Taking antibiotics for a viral illness does not cure it and can allow resistant bacteria to grow.

*Marking note: Up to 2 marks: 1 for saying antibiotics act on bacteria and not viruses, and 1 for saying viruses are different or live inside the body's cells. Do NOT accept 'because viruses are stronger'.*

### Question 19  [10 marks]

**(a)**

- Sustainable energy is energy from a source that will not run out, because it is replaced by nature as fast as people use it.
- Examples: the sun (solar energy), the wind, moving water behind a dam, biogas made from animal and food waste, heat from inside the earth.
- Accept also: a source that can be managed, such as wood from a plantation where new trees are planted as they are cut.

*Marking note: Up to 6 marks: up to 2 for the definition, 1 for 'will not run out' and 1 for the idea that nature replaces it; then 1 mark each for TWO correct examples, and 1 more for a third. Do NOT accept petrol, diesel, charcoal, coal, kerosene or LPG - none of these will last. Do NOT accept bare 'electricity', because it depends on the source it is made from.*

**(b)**

- Accept any sensible example, for instance: a cocoa or shea factory fits solar panels and a solar dryer, so it no longer burns firewood or runs a diesel dryer, which cuts smoke, cuts the carbon dioxide given out, and saves money on fuel.
- Or: a factory treats its waste water before it goes into the river, so the fish and the drinking water downstream are not poisoned.
- Or: a mill fits a more efficient motor and insulates its steam pipes, so it uses less electricity for the same amount of maize.
- Or: a mine or a large farm uses a biogas plant to turn its own waste into fuel.

*Marking note: Up to 4 marks: 1 for naming a real Ghanaian industry or a realistic one, 1 for the science or technology used, 1 for how it harms the environment less, and 1 for the result. A pupil who suggests something sensible but vague, such as 'use solar power', earns 2 at most. Give 0 for an answer that does not mention the environment.*

## QUALITY-CONTROL SUMMARY

*Every question was checked against the following fourteen points.*

**1. Does every question belong to the supplied curriculum scope?**

Yes. All 46 sub-questions trace to the Basic 7 Integrated Science curriculum, Strands 1 to 5, all 22 sub-strands, each carrying a content standard and an official indicator code.

**2. Is the paper appropriate for the stated class?**

Yes. Tasks ask for the number of points a JHS 1 pupil can reasonably produce in two hours - no more than four separate points in any one sub-question.

**3. Is the paper genuinely BECE-style?**

Yes: 20 essay questions with sub-questions, each carrying a printed mark allocation, in the BECE Paper 2 format.

**4. Are there enough application and reasoning questions?**

Yes. 48 marks are Apply, 2 Analyse, 6 Evaluate and 4 Create - 60 of 200 (30%). The Create item is Question 7(b), a fair-test experiment; the Evaluate items are 12(b) and 14(c).

**5. Are the marks for each question fitted to its sub-questions?**

Yes. Three-part questions carry 12; two-part questions carry 10 or 8 according to how much each part demands. Every N-mark sub-question asks for N things.

**6. Does each question's total match the sum of its parts?**

Yes. All 20 were machine-checked: the printed total equals the sum of the sub-question marks in every case.

**7. Are the marks internally consistent?**

Yes. The 20 totals are 12, 10, 10 and 8 repeated five times, giving 200, and the strand, sub-strand, cognitive and difficulty tables all agree.

**8. Does the total add up correctly?**

Yes: 200 marks, matching the figure printed on the front page.

**9. Are the answers in the marking scheme correct?**

Yes. All 46 sub-questions have expected answers and a marking note, each checked against the question it belongs to.

**10. Does the marking scheme give usable guidance?**

Yes. Every note covers partial credit, acceptable alternatives, common wrong answers and method marks. Notes that bite: the alimentary canal stops at the first sequence error; a food chain starting with an animal scores 0; 'sunlight' is not accepted for germination.

**11. Are the questions free from unnecessary ambiguity?**

Yes. Open items are anchored with 'name four', 'state three', 'explain TWO' and 'give ONE' plus the mark allocation, so a pupil can see exactly how much to write.

**12. Are Ghanaian contexts used naturally and accurately?**

Yes: one farmer with a single small plot, a village pond, unsafe stream water, a Ghanaian factory choosing its energy source, and local landforms.

**13. Has any content outside the curriculum been introduced?**

No. Every sub-question sits inside the 22 sub-strands, and nothing is tested that the B7 syllabus does not name.

**14. Are the questions sufficiently varied?**

Yes: explaining, comparing, describing a process, ordering, designing an experiment, evaluating a choice, tracing an energy change and justifying a decision.

## EXAMINER NOTES (for the teacher, not for candidates)

- Sit this paper separately from Paper 1. Two hours for 46 sub-questions is about two and a half minutes each, which is comfortable but not generous - pupils who rewrite the question will run out of time.

- The marks are fitted to the sub-questions: three-part questions carry 12, two-part questions carry 10 or 8. Tell the class this so they can budget their time by marks rather than by question count.

- Question 7(b) is the only Create item and the one pupils find hardest. They can name the conditions a seed needs but cannot set up a fair test. The marking note requires two groups, one difference, the expected result and a conclusion - teach that shape explicitly.

- Questions 12(b) and 14(c) are the Evaluate items and both are capped at 1 mark if the pupil makes a choice with no reason. There is no single right answer to either.

- Question 9(a) is marked in order and stops at the first part that is out of sequence - warn the class, it is the easiest place to lose 5 marks through carelessness.

- Question 11(b) scores 0 if the food chain begins with an animal, and 0 again if the arrows point the wrong way. The arrow means 'is eaten by' and the chain always starts with a plant.
