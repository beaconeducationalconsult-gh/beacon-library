- Also accept Marian Ewurama Addy, or Prof. Addy.
  - *Marking note: 2 marks for the correct name. 1 mark if only the surname 'Addy' is given with no first name. Do NOT accept Prof. Ibok Oduro, who is a food scientist and not a biochemist in the sense used here.*

**(iv)** [2 marks]

- Science education.
- Also accept science education, or the training of science teachers.
  - *Marking note: 2 marks for science education, or 1 mark for the vaguer 'education'. Do NOT accept 'teaching' alone at 2 marks - the field is specifically science education.*

### Question 5 (9 marks)

**(a)** [2 marks]

- Any two: scientific knowledge belongs to the whole world, so we use discoveries made in other countries every day; the medicines, machines and crops developed abroad are used in Ghana; their stories show pupils what patience, curiosity and hard work can achieve; learning about them gives pupils ideas they can apply to Ghana's own problems; understanding where knowledge comes from helps pupils to question and check things for themselves.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any TWO correct reasons (2 marks). Credit 'we use their discoveries here', 'they are role models' and 'their ideas help us solve our own problems'.*

**(b)** [4 marks]

- Accept any one correctly matched. Albert Einstein - physics; the theory of relativity and the equation E = mc squared. Alexander Fleming - medicine and microbiology; the discovery of penicillin, the first antibiotic. Charles Darwin - biology and natural history; the theory of evolution by natural selection. Stephen Hawking - physics and cosmology; his work on black holes and the nature of the universe. Marie Curie - physics and chemistry; her work on radioactivity. Isaac Newton - physics and mathematics; gravity and the laws of motion. Louis Pasteur - microbiology; showing that germs cause disease and developing vaccines.
  - *Marking note: Up to 4 marks: 1 mark for the name, 1 mark for the correct branch of science, and up to 2 marks for an accurate description of the contribution. Paul Ratnei is accepted as a name because he is named in the curriculum, but no contribution can be credited because none could be verified. Cap at 2 if the branch of science is right but the contribution is wrong or too vague, and give 0 if the contribution belongs to a different scientist.*

**(c)** [3 marks]

- Accept any accurate account of how the work named in (b) still matters, for example: Fleming's penicillin began the age of antibiotics, and antibiotics are still the treatment for bacterial infections in every Ghanaian hospital today, so without his work a simple cut or a chest infection could still kill.
- Or: Pasteur's idea that germs cause disease is the reason water is treated, milk is pasteurised, wounds are cleaned and children are vaccinated - all things done every day in Ghana now.
- Or: Einstein's work on energy and matter underlies the physics used in medical scanners, in satellite communication and in the generation of electricity.
  - *Marking note: Up to 3 marks. 1 mark for naming a present-day use; 1 mark for explaining that use clearly; and 1 mark for a Ghanaian or everyday detail that shows the pupil has really thought about it. Cap at 1 if the pupil only repeats the contribution from (b) without saying how it reaches us today.*

### Question 6 (6 marks)

**(a)** [2 marks]

- Any two: scientists publish their results so others can build on them; problems such as disease, climate and food do not stop at borders, so countries must work together; a discovery made in one country can save lives in another; sharing saves each country from repeating work that has already been done; international journals, conferences and the internet let results travel quickly.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any TWO correct reasons (2 marks). Credit 'results are published', 'problems cross borders' and 'it saves repeating work'.*

**(b)** [4 marks]

- Either position, with reasons. For training our own scientists: they understand Ghana's own problems and can work on them directly; the knowledge stays in the country and trains the next generation; it is cheaper in the long run than buying every solution from abroad; a country that cannot make or repair anything of its own is always dependent on others.
- For buying technology from abroad: it is quicker than waiting years to train people; some technology is very expensive to develop and already exists; and the two are not really opposites - a country can buy technology AND train the people who will maintain, repair and adapt it.
- A strong answer will say that Ghana needs both: to buy what exists and to train Ghanaians who can run it, repair it and eventually make their own.
  - *Marking note: Up to 4 marks. Credit marks for the REASONS, not for the position taken. 1 mark for a clear position, 1 mark for a developed reason, 1 mark for a second reason or for answering the other side, and 1 mark for recognising that Ghana needs both. A pupil who argues either side, or who says the two go together, can earn full marks if the reasoning is sound. Do NOT mark down a pupil for disagreeing with the examiner.*

### Question 7 (11 marks)

**(a)** [3 marks]

- Any three: electric light lets children read after dark; a refrigerator or a freezer keeps food and fish from spoiling; a mobile phone lets families keep in touch and lets traders check prices; mobile money lets people pay without carrying cash; a borehole with a hand pump gives clean water close to home; a grinding mill saves hours of pounding; a motorbike or a truck carries produce to market faster; gas or electric cookers have replaced some firewood.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct changes (3 marks). Each must name a technology AND say what changed because of it. Do NOT credit a bare list of gadgets with no change described.*

**(b)** [4 marks]

- HOME: a page listing the technologies used at home - a refrigerator, a mobile phone, electric light, a gas cooker - each with a picture or drawing and a note of what the family did before it had them.
- SCHOOL: a page on the technologies used in the school - computers, a projector, calculators, the science laboratory, treated water or a borehole - and what they have changed about lessons.
- COMMUNITY: a page on the technologies seen in the town or village - solar panels, a borehole, a mobile money agent, a grinding mill, a clinic, a tractor or improved storage for crops - with a note on who uses each one.
- A title page, a contents list, and a short conclusion at the end on what all of this has changed for the better.
  - *Marking note: Up to 4 marks. 1 mark for a clear page or section for the HOME, 1 mark for the SCHOOL, 1 mark for the COMMUNITY, and 1 mark for the overall presentation, such as a title page, a contents list and a conclusion. Credit only if all three of home, school and community are actually covered - cap at 1 if only one or two are, and cap at 2 if they are named but nothing is said about what goes on each page.*

**(c)** [4 marks]

- CHECKING: ask a parent, a teacher or an older person whether what you have written is true; compare what you found in one place with what a book or a trusted website says; check a fact found on the internet against a second source before writing it down; ask someone who uses the technology, such as the miller or the nurse, to confirm how it helps.
- PRESENTING: pin the pages on the classroom wall and walk the class round them; give a short talk to the class or to another class; demonstrate one of the technologies while it is being explained; show the album to parents on an open day; act out 'before and after' for one of the technologies.
  - *Marking note: Up to 4 marks, split into two halves. Up to 2 marks for CHECKING - 1 mark for each of two different ways of checking, or 2 marks for one way fully explained with a named person or source. Up to 2 marks for PRESENTING - 1 mark for each of two different ways, or 2 marks for one way fully described. An answer that covers only checking or only presenting cannot earn more than 2.*

### EXAMINER NOTES

- The curriculum names 'Paul Ratnei' among the international scientists in Exemplar 2. That name could not be found in any reference source and is very likely a typographical error in the curriculum document. No question here tests a contribution by him; he is accepted as a name in Question 5(b) and nowhere else, and the note says no contribution can be credited. If you know who was intended, teach the right name and accept it.
- Question 3 is the one that is easiest to mark wrongly. It asks for SCIENCE EDUCATORS, not research scientists, so Prof. Allotey and Prof. Oduro do not fit here even though they are the names pupils remember best. The science educators are Prof. Anamuah-Mensah, Prof. Ossei-Anto and Prof. Anthony-Krueger. Keep Question 3 and Question 4 apart when you teach them.
- Question 4(i) is the paper's most reliable discriminator. Prof. Allotey is MATHEMATICAL PHYSICS, not mathematics - the Allotey Formalism came from his work on soft X-ray spectroscopy, and he was the first Ghanaian full professor of mathematics but a physicist by research. The note gives 2 marks for mathematical physics and only 1 for 'physics' or 'mathematics' alone.
- Question 1(c) looks easy and is not. Pupils write 'a veterinary doctor treats animals' and earn 0, because the question asks how that improves HUMAN life. The two marks are for the link - food, income, ploughing, or protection from diseases that pass from animals to people. Teach the link, not the job.
- Question 6(b) is deliberately open, and the strongest answers say Ghana needs BOTH - to buy technology that already exists and to train Ghanaians who can run it, repair it and eventually make their own. Credit any position that is argued, and do not mark down a pupil who disagrees with you.
- Question 5(c) is the Analyse item and it is harder than it looks. Pupils tend to repeat the contribution from 5(b) instead of tracing it to today. The note caps them at 1 for that. Push them to a concrete present-day example - antibiotics in a Ghanaian hospital, clean water and vaccination from Pasteur's germ theory.
- Topic weighting is 28 marks for 18.1, 27 for 18.2, 24 for 18.3 and 21 for 18.4. If you spent longer on science, technology and innovation than on careers, move marks towards Question 7.
- Three cheap activities that raise marks sharply: (1) give each group one Ghanaian scientist to research for ten minutes and let them present to the class, which teaches Questions 3 and 4 and satisfies the 'presentations' in the exemplars; (2) hand out a page of job advertisements cut from a newspaper and ask pupils which ones need science and why, which teaches Question 1; and (3) walk round the school listing every piece of technology and asking what people did before it existed, which teaches Question 7(a).
