## ANSWER KEY AND MARKING SCHEME

**14.1  14.1 What is Force?**

- **Q1** (4 marks) (a) Non-contact. The magnet and the compass needle never touch - the magnet acts through the air and the case by its magnetic field. (b) Contact. Friction only arises where the sole of the foot and the surface of the tile are actually pressed together and rubbing. (c) Non-contact. Gravity pulls the coconut down through the air all the way to the ground with nothing touching it; it acts through the earth's gravitational field. (d) Contact. The blade has to be pushed right against the earth before it can push it - the force disappears the moment the blade is lifted clear.
- **Q2** (3 marks) A force is a push or a pull acting on an object; it can change the object's motion or its shape. Any two named forces: muscular (frictional), friction, gravitational (weight), magnetic, electrostatic, upthrust, tension.

**14.2  14.2 Balanced and Unbalanced Forces**

- **Q3** (3 marks) 1. It STOPS the ball - the ball was moving and the force from his gloves brings it to rest. 2. It CHANGES THE DIRECTION of the ball - if he palms it away rather than holding it, the force deflects it along a new path. 3. It CHANGES THE SHAPE of the ball - the ball squashes against his gloves for the moment of the catch, then springs back. All three come from the same single blow, which is the point the diagram makes.

**14.3  14.3 Inertia**

- **Q4** (4 marks) Where it helps - walking. To move forward you push backwards against the ground with your foot. Friction opposes the tendency of your foot to slide backwards, so it gives you something to push against and you move forward. On a wet or polished floor there is little friction, your foot slides, and you cannot walk properly. Where it wastes - the bearings in a bicycle wheel or a car engine. The metal surfaces rub as they turn, friction opposes the turning, and the work done against it is converted into heat. Energy that came from the rider's muscles or from the fuel ends up warming the bearings instead of moving the machine. That is why bearings are greased, and why they wear out and have to be replaced.
- **Q5** (3 marks) Reducing, any two: put oil or grease between the surfaces; use wheels, rollers or ball bearings so the surfaces roll instead of sliding; make the surfaces smoother; separate them with a cushion of air as a hovercraft does. Increasing, any two: make the surfaces rougher, as with sandpaper or a tyre tread; press the surfaces together harder, as a heavier load does; use a material that grips more, such as rubber.

**14.4  14.4 Newton's First Law of Motion**

- **Q6** (4 marks) The two forces on the book are equal in size and opposite in direction, so they cancel each other: the resultant force on the book is zero. A resultant of zero means no change in motion, so the book stays exactly as it is - at rest. It is not that no forces act on it; two do. It is that together they add to nothing. Take the table away and the upward push disappears. The forces are now unbalanced: 6 N downwards with nothing opposing it. The book therefore accelerates downwards - it falls, and it goes faster the longer it falls.
- **Q7** (3 marks) The resultant is 800 N - 800 N = 0 N. The pulls are equal and opposite, so they cancel and the rope stays at rest. When team A begins to win, their pull has become greater than team B's. The forces are now unbalanced, there is a resultant in A's direction, and the rope accelerates towards team A. It is not that team B has stopped pulling - B's pull is simply now the smaller of the two.

**14.5  14.5 Applications of Newton's First Law**

- **Q8** (4 marks) Acceleration is a change in velocity, and velocity is not the same thing as speed: velocity includes the direction of motion. The stone's speed is constant, but its direction is changing at every instant, because to stay on the circle it must be turning all the time. So its velocity is continually changing, and therefore it is accelerating - even though nothing about the speed changes. Because the direction turns towards the centre, the acceleration is towards the centre, and so there must be a resultant force towards the centre. Here that force is the tension in the string, pulling inward along its length. Cut the string and the force vanishes; the stone then stops turning and goes straight on along the tangent, which is exactly what the diagram shows.

**14.6  14.6 Magnets: Types and Characteristics**

- **Q9** (4 marks) Nothing is pushing her to the right. Before the turn, she and the bus are both moving straight ahead. As the bus turns left, the floor and whatever she is holding push on her and turn her left too - that inward push is the centripetal force, and it is what makes her follow the curve instead of continuing straight. But her body tends, by inertia, to keep going in the original straight line, so relative to the turning bus she drifts towards the right-hand side and ends up pressed against it. What she feels as an outward 'centrifugal force' is simply her own body trying to carry straight on while the bus turns underneath her. The real force on her is inward, towards the centre of the turn - the outward feeling is an effect of her own inertia, not a force at all.
- **Q10** (3 marks) A centripetal force is a REAL force acting towards the centre of the circle - the tension in a string, the pull of gravity, or the grip of tyres on a road - and it is what keeps an object moving in a circle instead of going straight. The centrifugal force is NOT a real force at all: it is the apparent outward effect you feel because your body tends to keep moving in a straight line while whatever is carrying you turns inward beneath you.

**14.7  14.7 Repulsion, Attraction and Orientation**

- **Q11** (3 marks) The drum spins the clothes round in a circle at high speed. To keep something moving in a circle there must be a force pulling it inward, and for the clothes that force comes from the drum wall pushing on them. But the water in the cloth is only loosely held, and the drum has holes in it, so the wall cannot push on the water. With no inward force to hold it in the circle, the water simply continues in a straight line - out through the holes and into the outer casing. The faster the drum spins, the greater the inward force the water would need, and so the more of it is thrown out.

**14.8  14.8 Uses of Magnets**

- **Q12** (3 marks) It is falling - continuously. Gravity is the inward force that bends its path, and it never stops acting on the satellite. But the satellite also has a very large sideways speed, and as it falls towards the earth the earth's surface curves away beneath it at just the same rate, so the satellite keeps missing the ground. Gravity curves its path into a closed loop around the earth rather than letting it fly off in a straight line, and with no air up there to slow it down it keeps its speed. An orbit is a permanent fall that never lands.

**PRACTICAL TASK**

- **Q13** (3 marks) 1. A rougher surface gives more friction. With no load on the block, the bare bench gives 2.0 N but sandpaper gives 4.6 N - more than twice as much for the same block and the same load. Oiling the bench cuts it to 1.1 N, which is lower than the bare bench. 2. A heavier load gives more friction. On the bare bench the reading rises from 2.0 N with no load to 3.1 N with 1 kg on top and 4.3 N with 2 kg - roughly doubling as the load goes up. A third conclusion: rollers almost remove friction entirely, cutting the reading from 2.0 N to 0.3 N.
- **Q14** (3 marks) If the block is moving at a steady speed its motion is not changing, so the forces on it must be balanced: the pull forwards is exactly equal to the friction backwards, and the balance reading therefore IS the friction. If the block were accelerating, the forces would no longer be balanced - the pull would have to be greater than the friction in order to produce the acceleration. The reading would then be higher than the true friction, and it would not settle to a steady value because the block would keep gaining speed.
- **Q15** (3 marks) Any two, each explained. The same block must be used, pulled on the same face, so that the area in contact is the same in every trial. The pull must be horizontal in each case - if the balance is tilted, part of the pull goes into lifting the block rather than overcoming friction, and the reading is not the friction. The same load must be used when comparing one surface with another. The balance should be checked to read zero before each trial, and read at eye level to avoid a parallax error.

## EXAMINER NOTES

- Pitched at the compulsory diagram question of BECE Paper 2: 76% of the marks are at Apply level or above and 38% are Challenging.
- Q8 is the best question here, and the one that decides whether a pupil understands circular motion or has merely memorised the word. The whole answer hangs on one distinction: speed is how fast, velocity is how fast AND which way. A stone on a string has constant speed and constantly changing velocity, so it is accelerating. Pupils who say 'its speed is constant so there is no acceleration' have the wrong definition, not a missing fact.
- Q9 on the turning bus is the same idea from inside. Nothing pushes the passenger outwards. She keeps going straight; the bus turns in underneath her. If a pupil finishes the answer still believing a centrifugal force pushed her, they have not got it - and this is the single most common misconception in the whole strand.
- Q12 on the satellite is worth doing aloud at the board. 'It is falling and missing' is the sentence to aim for; it is short, it is correct, and it is memorable in a way that 'gravity is balanced by centrifugal force' is not - that answer is wrong and very common.
- Q4 on friction both helping and wasting is where pupils show whether they can argue both sides. Most can give one example of friction being a nuisance (wearing out soles) but cannot say what it does when it helps, which is: give you something to push against.
- The friction experiment needs a spring balance reading to at least 0.1 N, a wooden block with a hook, and a set of masses. Reading the balance while the block is actually moving at a steady speed is the awkward part - a slow, steady pull takes practice, and it is worth letting each group have several attempts before recording.
- If spring balances are short, the whole class can work from one balance passed round, or the four surfaces can be tested by different groups and the results pooled on the board.
