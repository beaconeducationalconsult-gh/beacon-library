## ANSWER KEY AND MARKING SCHEME

**4.1  4.1 What is a Life Cycle?**

- **Q1** (4 marks) It is a cycle because it comes back to where it started: the adult produces eggs, the eggs become young, the young grow and change, and they become adults that produce eggs again. There is no real beginning or end - any stage can be a starting point, and each generation repeats what the last one did. A straight line would have a start and a finish, which is wrong. If the loop were broken at any point the species would end: if the adults died before laying eggs there would be no eggs; if the eggs did not hatch there would be no young; if the young did not survive to become adults there would be nothing to lay the next batch of eggs. Every stage is essential because every stage produces the next one.
- **Q2** (2 marks) Adult, eggs, young (larva or nymph), and growing and changing. Any order, one mark for each two named correctly.

**4.2  4.2 Stages of the Life Cycle of the Housefly: Egg → Larva → Pupa → Adult**

- **Q3** (4 marks) A maggot has to moult because it is wrapped in an outer skin, the cuticle, which is fairly rigid and does not grow. As the body inside gets bigger the skin becomes too tight, so the larva splits it open and wriggles out in a newer, larger one underneath - and it does this twice. It is a COMPLETE metamorphosis because there are four clearly separate stages - egg, larva, pupa and adult - and the larva looks nothing like the adult: it has no wings, no legs, no compound eyes, and it lives and feeds in a completely different way. The change is not just growth, it is a rebuilding of the body, and most of that rebuilding happens out of sight inside the pupal case. In an incomplete metamorphosis the young resemble small adults and simply get bigger.
- **Q4** (3 marks) Adding up the times, a whole generation takes only about seven to twelve days: up to a day as an egg, three to five days as a larva, and three to six days as a pupa. So in roughly two weeks every egg has become an adult. And each female lays up to 150 eggs at a time, so one heap does not produce one fly - it produces dozens or hundreds, and every one of those new females immediately lays her own 150. The numbers multiply generation by generation, which is why the flies seem to appear all at once rather than gradually.

**4.3  4.3 Feeding Habits of the Housefly**

- **Q5** (4 marks) The fly has a sponging proboscis and no teeth or jaws, so it cannot bite and it cannot chew. That single fact drives everything that follows. Because it cannot take in solid food, it has to turn its food into a liquid first: it spits saliva onto whatever it has landed on, soaks it up, and sponges the liquid in. So to feed at all, the fly must make close contact with the surface of its food - and when that surface is faeces or rubbish, germs stick to the hairs on its legs and body and to its mouthparts. It then flies to the next meal, often our food, and does exactly the same thing there. The mouthparts are the reason the fly must touch filth and then touch food; the feeding method IS the transmission mechanism. A biting insect would inject germs straight into the blood - the fly simply walks them onto what we are about to eat.
- **Q6** (3 marks) Because it cannot bite or chew - it has no jaws and no teeth, only a sponging proboscis with a soft pad at the end. That pad can take up liquid but cannot bite pieces off solid food or grind them. So before it can feed on anything solid the fly has to dissolve it: it pours saliva over the food to soften and liquefy it, and then sponges up the liquid that results.

**4.4  4.4 How the Housefly Affects Human Health**

- **Q7** (4 marks) A fly lands on faeces, rubbish or a corpse, all of which teem with germs, and some of those germs stick to the hairs on its legs and body. It then flies off and lands on food - often cooked food left uncovered on a table - and walks over it, leaving the germs behind as it feeds and as it simply treads on it. A person then eats that food without reheating it and swallows the germs, which multiply in the gut and cause illness. Any one of these steps can be broken: cover the food so the fly cannot reach it; dispose of faeces in a latrine so the fly cannot reach them in the first place; screen the windows so flies cannot get in; or reheat food thoroughly before eating it.
- **Q8** (3 marks) The fly does not cause cholera and cannot give it to anyone on its own - it carries the bacterium that does. The disease is caused by the germ, Vibrio cholerae, which comes originally from the faeces of an infected person. The fly is only the vehicle: it picks the germ up on its legs and body and delivers it to food, without being infected itself. This is called mechanical transmission. The difference matters directly for control: if the fly were the cause, killing flies would end cholera. Because it is only one route among several, a household can be completely free of flies and still get cholera from contaminated water.

**4.5  4.5 Diseases Spread by the Housefly**

- **Q9** (3 marks) Typhoid. A vaccine works by preparing the immune system against one particular organism, and typhoid vaccine prepares it against the typhoid bacterium only. Cholera and dysentery are caused by different organisms, so the typhoid vaccine gives no protection against either of them. Those two are prevented in other ways - safe drinking water, washing hands with soap, using a latrine, and keeping food covered.
- **Q10** (3 marks) Any two symptoms: passing faeces containing blood and mucus; painful cramps in the abdomen; frequent, urgent passing of stool; fever. Any one prevention: dispose of faeces safely in a latrine so flies cannot reach them; wash hands with soap after using the toilet and before handling food; drink safe, clean water; keep cooked food covered.

**4.6  4.6 How to Reduce the Effects of Housefly Activities**

- **Q11** (4 marks) Spraying kills only the adult flies that are alive at that moment. The eggs, the larvae and the pupae are all still in the rubbish, sheltered inside the rotting material and, in the pupa's case, sealed inside a hard case that the spray cannot reach. Within a week those develop into adults and the population is back where it started - so spraying has to be repeated endlessly and only ever thins the flies temporarily. Removing breeding places - covered bins, latrines, clearing away rotting matter - means the next generation is never produced at all. The population falls and stays down. Killing the adults treats the symptom; denying them somewhere to breed treats the cause.
- **Q12** (3 marks) Screening the windows - and doors - with fine mesh or netting. The holes in the mesh are smaller than a housefly, so a fly cannot pass through them, but they are large enough for air to move through freely and for light to come in. So the house stays cool, lit and ventilated, but the flies are kept outside where they cannot reach the food.

**PRACTICAL TASK**

- **Q13** (4 marks) Houseflies breed on rotting organic matter that they are able to reach. Jar A, rotting fruit left open, produced many maggots, so rotting fruit is a suitable breeding material. Jar C, fresh cooked rice left open, produced none, so being reachable is not enough - the material has to be rotting. Jar D, dry soil left open, produced none, because there was no food in it for a larva to eat. And Jar B, the same rotting fruit but covered with net, produced none, showing that the flies must actually reach the material to lay eggs on it. Taken together: rotting matter, within reach of a fly.
- **Q14** (3 marks) Jar B contains exactly the same rotting fruit as Jar A, in the same kind of jar, left in the same place for the same five days - and it produced no maggots at all. The only difference between the two jars is the net over B that stopped flies from landing on the fruit. If rotting matter turned into maggots by itself, Jar B would have produced them just as Jar A did. Since it did not, the maggots cannot come from the fruit: they must come from eggs laid by flies that reached it. Jar B is the control that rules the prediction out.
- **Q15** (3 marks) Any two, each explained. All four jars must be the same size and type, so that no jar gives the flies a bigger target or a different microclimate. They must all be left in the same place for the same five days, because a warmer or sunnier spot would speed up hatching and make that jar look like a better breeding site when it is only a warmer one. The same amount of material should be put in each jar. Whatever is used to cover Jar B must not shade it or change its temperature. Any of these differences would give a second explanation for the result, and the experiment would no longer show what it claims to show.

## EXAMINER NOTES

- Pitched at the compulsory diagram question of BECE Paper 2: 72% of the marks are at Apply level or above and 38% are Challenging.
- Q5 is the best question in the paper. Pupils know the fly spreads disease; almost none can say WHY. The answer is the mouthparts: it cannot bite, so it must liquefy its food, so it must make contact with filth and then with our food. The feeding method is the transmission mechanism. Pupils who get this have connected 4.3 to 4.4.
- Q8 on 'the fly gives you cholera' is the companion to Q5. The distinction between cause and vehicle matters because it changes what control can achieve: a fly-free house can still have cholera from dirty water.
- Q11 on why spraying is less effective than removing breeding sites is the question that tests whether 4.6 has been understood or merely listed. Pupils like spraying because it is dramatic; the answer is that it leaves the next generation untouched.
- Q14 is the most valuable question in the practical. The belief that rotting matter turns into maggots is extremely common and extremely persistent, and Jar B is exactly the control that kills it. Do not let this one go by - ask the class to predict BEFORE the jars are opened, then hold them to it.
- The jar experiment in Fig. 7 costs nothing to run: four jars, some fruit, some net or muslin, and five days. Jar B must be covered with something flies cannot pass but air can. Run it in the last week before the topic so the results are ready to discuss.
- Note the timing arithmetic in Q4: egg plus larva plus pupa is about 7-12 days, so two weeks is one to two generations. Pupils who add the numbers get the answer; those who guess 'because there is a lot of rubbish' do not.
