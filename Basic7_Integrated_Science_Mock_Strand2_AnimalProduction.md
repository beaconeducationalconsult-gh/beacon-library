  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). Accept any observation a pupil could really make: chewing the cud is the strongest one. Award at least 1 mark for naming the goat as the ruminant.*

### Question 6 (11 marks)

**(a)** [2 marks]

- An animal by-product is a useful material obtained from an animal apart from the main product.
- It is something useful that comes from the animal but is not the meat, milk or eggs the animal is mainly kept for.
- It is what is left after the main product has been taken, and it is still useful.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any TWO correct points (2 marks). The key idea is 'apart from the main product'. Award 1 mark for 'something useful we get from an animal besides the main one'.*

**(b)** [5 marks]

- Dung: used as manure to add plant nutrients to the soil.
- Bones: processed into bone meal for animal feed and fertilizer.
- Hides and skins: made into leather for shoes, bags and belts.
- Feathers: used to stuff pillows, cushions and mattresses.
- Blood: dried into blood meal and used in animal feed.
- Horns and hooves: made into buttons, glue, combs and ornaments.
- Fat (tallow): used to make soap and candles.
- Wool and hair: spun into cloth, blankets and carpets.
- Urine: mixed with dung and crop waste to make compost.
- Eggshells: crushed and added to poultry feed or compost.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark for each by-product correctly named with a sensible importance (5 marks). Both the name and the importance must be right for the mark. Accept any by-product really used in Ghana.*

**(c)** [4 marks]

- Dung is important because it adds plant nutrients to the soil and improves the soil structure, so crops grow better.
- It also helps the soil to hold water, which matters during the dry season and the harmattan.
- Using dung as manure saves the farmer money that would be spent on fertilizer.
- Before use the dung should be heaped and left to rot (composted) for some weeks.
- Composting kills some of the harmful organisms and weed seeds, and makes the nutrients easier for the crop to use.
- The rotted dung should then be spread on the bed and worked lightly into the soil, or placed near the roots and covered with soil.
- Fresh dung should not be placed straight on a crop, because it can scorch young plants.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any FOUR correct points (4 marks). The answer must say something about BOTH why dung is important AND how it is treated; award at most 2 marks if only one of these is dealt with.*

### Question 7 (5 marks)

**(a)** [3 marks]

- Decide on the questions the class wants answered, e.g. which animals are kept and what each is used for.
- Walk round the community (compounds, kraals, coops, the market) and observe the animals.
- Write down each animal seen, how many there are, and what the owner keeps it for.
- Ask the owners a few set questions, and write the answers down carefully.
- Record the information in a table: animal, group (ruminant, monogastric or poultry), domestic uses, commercial uses and by-products.
- Take drawings or photographs if the class is allowed to, and label them.
- Bring the results together, discuss them in groups and write a short report.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). This is a design task: award method marks for any sensible step. Look first for observing the animals, recording what is found in an orderly way, and asking the owners.*

**(b)** [2 marks]

- It helps the class to see which animals are really kept in their own community and what they are used for.
- It makes the work in class real, instead of only reading about animals in a book.
- It shows the class which animals earn money for families, so they understand commercial use.
- It helps the class to practise observing, recording and working in a group.
- The class could advise families on better ways of caring for their animals.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any TWO sensible reasons (2 marks). Accept any reason that shows the study was worth doing. Do NOT accept 'it is fun' on its own.*

### EXAMINER NOTES

- This sub-strand has TWO content standards, so the blueprint carries both: B7/JHS1.2.4.1 for topics 6.1-6.3 (differences among domestic animals) and B7/JHS1.2.4.2 for topics 6.4-6.5 (usefulness for domestic and commercial purposes). Each blueprint row shows the standard that applies to it.
- Follow the curriculum's wording on the three groups: a ruminant has a FOUR-CHAMBERED stomach and chews the cud; a monogastric animal has a SIMPLE, single-chambered stomach; and poultry are birds described as monogastric herbivores, because they have a simple stomach and feed mainly on plant material such as grain (A14).
- Question 3(a) turns 'discuss and write the differences' into a completion table with six numbered blanks, (i) to (vi), so marking is quick and fair: 1 mark each, and the blank number must be matched to the answer.
- Question 5(c) is the poster exemplar (B7/JHS1.2.4.2.1, Ex. 2) and Question 7 is the community-study exemplar (B7/JHS1.2.4.2.2, Ex. 1). Both are design tasks, so award method marks: any sensible feature or step earns credit. If your class actually makes the poster or does the study, accept what they did as evidence.
- Topic 6.1 asks pupils to match animals with their breeds. Keep breeds gentle: N'Dama, Sanga and Zebu for cattle, West African Dwarf and Sahelian for goats, Djallonke for sheep. Accept local names.
- Topics 6.4 and 6.5 carry 24 marks each, the highest weightings, because the second content standard is entirely about uses and by-products. If you want a flatter spread, move marks from Questions 4, 5 and 6 to Questions 1 and 2.
- A20 and A23 ask why a ruminant can use grass. The expected level is 'micro-organisms in the ruminant stomach break down the fibre' - do not expect any detail about rumen anatomy or about microbial protein.
