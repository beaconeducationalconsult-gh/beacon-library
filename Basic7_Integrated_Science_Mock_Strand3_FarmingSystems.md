### Question 1 (8 marks)

**(a)** [2 marks]

- A farming system is the particular way in which a farmer organises the land, the crops and the animals.
- It is the method or plan a farmer follows in using the land to produce crops and animals.
- It is the pattern of farming a farmer chooses, e.g. land rotation or mixed farming.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any TWO correct points (2 marks). Award 1 mark for 'the way a farmer farms' if it is not developed further. Do NOT accept 'a type of crop'.*

**(b)** [3 marks]

- Land rotation
- Crop rotation
- Mixed cropping
- Mixed farming
- Organic farming
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct farming systems (3 marks). Accept any three of the five. Do NOT accept 'irrigation farming', 'mechanised farming' or 'subsistence farming' - those are not the five named in the curriculum.*

**(c)** [3 marks]

- Land rotation needs a lot of land, so it only suits a place where land is plentiful; crop rotation and mixed cropping suit a small plot.
- The crops a farmer wants decide the system: some crops do well in a rotation, others are better mixed.
- Mixed farming needs money to buy and feed the animals, so a poor farmer may not afford it.
- Organic farming needs labour and bulky manure rather than money, so it suits a farmer with little cash.
- A farmer with a small plot and no money would choose mixed cropping with organic manure, not land rotation.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). The answer must mention the land, the crops AND what the farmer can afford to earn full marks; award at most 2 marks if only one or two of these are dealt with.*

### Question 2 (9 marks)

**(a)** [3 marks]

- Land rotation is the system in which the farmer farms a piece of land for a few seasons and then leaves it to rest and moves to another piece.
- The abandoned land is left fallow for several years so that it can regain its fertility.
- It is also called shifting cultivation.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). The key ideas are: farm, abandon, move on, and let the land rest.*

**(b)** [3 marks]

- It lets the soil regain its fertility while the land rests.
- It needs no artificial fertilizer, so it costs very little money.
- It is simple: the farmer needs no special knowledge or expensive tools.
- It suits a place where there is plenty of land and few people.
- It lets the bush grow back, which helps to restore the land.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct reasons (3 marks). Accept 'the land rests and becomes fertile again' as one reason.*

**(c)** [3 marks]

- It needs a very large area of land.
- Trees and forest are destroyed each time a new piece of land is cleared.
- The abandoned land can be eroded by wind and rain because nothing holds the soil.
- The farmer has to keep moving, so he cannot build a permanent home or store.
- It cannot feed many people on a small area of land.
- Burning the cleared vegetation pollutes the air and destroys the humus in the soil.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct problems (3 marks). The two things to look for first are the large area of land needed and the destruction of trees.*

### Question 3 (9 marks)

**(a)** [3 marks]

- Crop rotation is growing different crops one after another in a planned order on the same piece of land.
- The crop is changed from season to season or from year to year according to a plan.
- The same land is used every season, but not for the same crop.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). The key ideas are: different crops, in order, on the SAME land.*

**(b)** [3 marks]

- A sensible three-season rotation, e.g. maize, then a legume such as groundnuts or beans, then cassava or a vegetable.
- The legume goes in the middle because it puts nitrogen back into the soil, which the maize took out.
- A crop with a different root depth follows, so the nutrients are taken from a different layer of soil.
- Changing the crop breaks the life cycle of the pests and diseases that live on maize.
- Accept any sensible order, provided the pupil says WHY the crops are in that order.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark for a sensible three-crop rotation and 1 mark each for any TWO correct reasons for the order (3 marks). This is a design task: award method marks for any sensible plan that is explained. The rotation must change the crop each season.*

**(c)** [3 marks]

- The soil stays fertile, because different crops take different nutrients and some put nutrients back.
- Pests and diseases that live on one crop die out when that crop is not grown.
- The soil structure improves, because different crops have different roots.
- The farmer gets a variety of food and produce instead of only maize.
- The same piece of land can be used every year, instead of being abandoned.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct advantages (3 marks). Accept 'the soil does not get tired'.*

### Question 4 (8 marks)

**(a)** [2 marks]

- Mixed cropping is growing two or more different crops together on the same piece of land at the same time.
- The crops are planted together and grow together on one plot.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any TWO correct points (2 marks). The key ideas are: two or more crops, together, at the SAME time. Do NOT accept crop rotation here.*

**(b)** [3 marks]

- The maize uses the top layer of soil while the cassava and cocoyam feed deeper, so the whole plot is used.
- If one crop fails, the others may still do well, so the farmer is sure of some food.
- The family gets a variety of food - maize, cassava and cocoyam - from one small plot.
- The leaves cover the soil, which keeps the moisture in, keeps the weeds down and stops the soil being washed away.
- The tall maize and the low cocoyam use the sunlight at different heights.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). Accept any sensible benefit of growing these three crops together.*

**(c)** [3 marks]

- The crops compete with one another for light, water and soil nutrients.
- It is difficult to weed, to spray and to harvest, and harder to use machines.
- The yield of each crop is often lower than if it were grown alone.
- It is difficult to apply fertilizer to one crop without it reaching the others.
- Some crops may shade the others too much if the spacing is wrong.
- It is harder to tell how well each crop is doing.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct problems (3 marks). The two things to look for first are competition between the crops and the difficulty of working the plot.*

### Question 5 (8 marks)

**(a)** [3 marks]

- Mixed farming is the system in which the farmer keeps animals and grows crops on the same farm.
- The crops and the animals are reared together on one piece of land.
- For example, growing maize and cassava while keeping goats, sheep, cattle or poultry.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). The key idea is CROPS AND ANIMALS TOGETHER. Do NOT accept mixed cropping here.*

**(b)** [2 marks]

- The animals give droppings which are used as manure on the crops.
- The crops give waste and leftover parts - stalks, leaves, peelings and bran - which are used to feed the animals.
- Animals such as cattle can also be used to plough the land before planting.
- The animals graze the crop waste after harvest and clear the land for the next planting.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any TWO correct points (2 marks). One point should show the animals helping the crops and one the crops helping the animals.*

**(c)** [3 marks]

- The system is MIXED FARMING.
- The animals may damage or eat the crops if they are not fenced, tethered or housed.
- It needs more land, more labour, more capital and more knowledge than growing crops alone.
- The animals need water and care every day of the year, even in the dry season.
- Disease can kill the animals and so destroy that part of the farm's income.
- The farmer must find feed and housing for the animals as well as tending the crops.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark for naming mixed farming and 1 mark each for any TWO correct problems (3 marks). The name must be given for the first mark.*

**(b)** [2 marks]

- Compost
- Animal manure (poultry manure, cow dung, goat droppings)
- Green manure
- Crop residue
- Wood ash
- Neem extract
- Decayed leaves
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any TWO correct materials (2 marks). Do NOT accept urea, NPK or any manufactured fertilizer.*

**(c)** [3 marks]

- The yield may be lower, at least in the first few seasons, so the farmer earns less.
- It needs a lot of bulky material - compost and manure are heavy and difficult to carry and spread.
- It needs more labour: weeding by hand and hand-picking pests takes time.
- The results take longer to show than with artificial fertilizer.
- Pests and diseases are harder to control without chemicals.
- It is harder to farm a large area this way.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). Accept 'it is harder work' and 'the yield is lower'.*

### Question 7 (10 marks)

**(a)** [6 marks]

- (i) farms a piece of land for a few seasons, then leaves it to rest and moves to another piece
- (ii) it needs a very large area of land (and trees are destroyed when new land is cleared)
- (iii) it needs careful planning and knowledge of which crop should follow which
- (iv) grows two or more different crops together on the same piece of land at the same time
- (v) keeps animals and grows crops on the same farm
- (vi) the animals may damage the crops if they are not fenced or tethered (it also needs more land, labour and capital)
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for all SIX blanks correctly filled (6 marks). The blank number must be matched to the correct answer. Accept any clear equivalent wording.*

**(b)** [4 marks]

- Crop rotation changes the crop over time - one crop after another - while mixed cropping grows several crops together at the same time.
- In crop rotation only one crop occupies the land at a time; in mixed cropping two or more crops share the plot.
- Crop rotation is a plan across seasons or years; mixed cropping is a plan for one season.
- Crop rotation keeps the soil fertile mainly by varying the nutrients taken; mixed cropping uses different root depths and different layers of the soil.
- Crop rotation is easier to weed, spray and harvest; mixed cropping is harder to work, especially with machines.
- Crop rotation guards against pests by changing the host plant; mixed cropping guards against the failure of one crop.
- In crop rotation the yield of the single crop is usually higher; in mixed cropping each crop may yield less.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any FOUR correct differences (4 marks). Each point must contrast the two systems, not just describe one of them. The single most important difference is time: one after another versus at the same time.*

### EXAMINER NOTES

- The single most common error in this topic is the swap between MIXED CROPPING (several crops together at the same time) and CROP ROTATION (different crops one after another on the same land). Question 7(b) is written to test exactly that, and the marking note insists that each point must contrast the two systems, not just describe one of them.
- The second most common error is the swap between MIXED CROPPING (crops + crops) and MIXED FARMING (crops + animals). Question 5(c) makes pupils name the system before they earn the marks.
- Question 3(b) is a design task: a three-season rotation. Award method marks - any sensible order earns credit provided the pupil says WHY the crops are in that order. Putting a legume in the middle is the answer to look for.
- Question 7(a) is the comparison table. The six blanks are numbered (i) to (vi), so marking is quick and fair: 1 mark each, and the blank number must be matched to the answer.
- Topic weightings are deliberately even here - every system carries 13 to 15 marks - because the indicator asks for the differences AMONG the systems rather than for depth on any one of them. If you want to stress one system, move marks towards Questions 2 to 6.
- B7 does not expect the words 'shifting cultivation' or 'slash-and-burn'. Accept them if a pupil offers them, but the expected answer is that the farmer moves on and leaves the land to rest.
