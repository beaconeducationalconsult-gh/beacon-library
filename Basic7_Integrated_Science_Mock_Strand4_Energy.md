### Question 6 (8 marks)

**(a)** [3 marks]

- Conduction - heat passes through a solid from particle to particle; the particles vibrate but do not move from place to place.
- Convection - heat is carried through a liquid or a gas by the liquid or the gas itself moving.
- Radiation - heat travels as waves and needs no material at all; it can cross empty space.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for all THREE correct methods (3 marks). One mark is for naming each method; if the pupil names the methods correctly but does not say what moves, award 2 marks in all.*

**(b)** [3 marks]

- Fix one end of a metal rod (or a long iron nail / a metal spoon) in a flame or put it in hot water and hold the other end.
- Stick drawing pins or candle wax / margarine along the rod at equal distances.
- Watch the far end of the rod - after a short time it also becomes hot and the pins drop off one after another, starting from the hot end.
- The heat has travelled along the metal from the hot end to the cold end, which shows conduction.
- Repeat with a wooden stick or a plastic rod as a control - the far end stays cool.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark for a sensible set-up, 1 mark for what would be seen / measured and 1 mark for the conclusion that heat travels along the metal (3 marks). This is a design task: award method marks for any workable plan that is clearly described.*

**(c)** [2 marks]

- The method is radiation.
- The sun's heat reaches the car as radiation, crossing the air and the glass without heating the air much on the way.
- The glass and the seats absorb the radiation and become hot, and they then warm the air inside.
- The warm air cannot escape from the closed car, so the heat is trapped and the inside gets hotter and hotter.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark for naming radiation and 1 mark for any ONE correct reason (2 marks). The key point is that radiation needs no material and that the heat is then trapped inside the car.*

### Question 7 (8 marks)

**(a)** [3 marks]

- Take three pieces of cardboard and make a small hole in the middle of each one.
- Stand the three cards in a straight line so that the three holes are in line with one another.
- Put a lit candle or a torch behind the first card and look through the third hole.
- The flame can be seen only when all three holes are in one straight line.
- Move one card sideways and the flame can no longer be seen - this shows that light cannot bend round the corner, so it travels in a straight line.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark for a sensible set-up, 1 mark for what would be seen and 1 mark for the conclusion (3 marks). Accept any correct straight-line demonstration, for example looking along the edge of a ruler or using a long straight pipe. Award method marks for a workable plan.*

**(b)** [2 marks]

- Reflection is the bouncing back of light when it falls on a shiny or polished surface, such as a mirror.
- Refraction is the bending of light as it passes from one material into another, for example from air into water or from air into glass.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for a correct explanation of reflection and of refraction (2 marks). The key word for reflection is 'bouncing back' and for refraction is 'bending'. Do NOT accept the two definitions swapped.*

**(c)** [3 marks]

- The process is called dispersion.
- Sunlight (white light) is made up of seven colours mixed together.
- When white light passes into a raindrop it is refracted (bent) and each colour bends by a different amount.
- The colours therefore separate out and come out of the drop travelling in slightly different directions.
- The seven colours - red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo and violet - appear as a rainbow.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark for naming dispersion and 1 mark each for any TWO correct points (3 marks). The idea to look for is that white light is a mixture of colours which are separated. Accept the explanation in terms of a glass prism instead of raindrops.*

### EXAMINER NOTES

- Questions 6(b) and 7(a) are the two design tasks, and they come straight from the exemplars - 'carry out an activity to show how heat is transferred' and 'perform experiments to show that light travels in a straight line'. Award method marks generously: any workable plan that is clearly described deserves credit, even if the apparatus is not the one in the scheme.
- Question 4 is the calculation question, and it is marked in three steps - formula, substitution, answer - so a pupil who forgets to square the 20 in KE = 1/2 mv2 still earns the formula mark. Remind pupils to write the formula first and to show the substitution, because an answer alone loses two marks.
- The single most common error in this sub-strand is saying that potential energy 'finishes' or 'is used up' as a body falls. Question 3(b) is written to catch it, and the marking note allows at most 2 of 3 marks if the pupil says the energy disappears.
- Question 2(a) is the completion table. The six blanks are numbered (i) to (vi), so marking is quick and fair: 1 mark each, and the blank number must be matched to the answer.
- Watch for the pair of confusions in Question 5(a): a kerosene lamp USES chemical energy even though it PRODUCES light, and a solar calculator USES solar energy. The note tells you which answer to accept for each.
- Topic weightings are deliberately even here - every topic carries 14 or 15 marks - because the content standard covers three separate ideas (forms of energy, heat transfer, and light). If your scheme of work spent longer on one of them, move marks towards Questions 2 to 4, 6 or 7.
- B7 does not expect pupils to state the law of conservation of energy; that is Sub-strand 3 (B7/JHS1.4.3.1.1). Here they only need to say that PE changes into KE, which Question 3(b) and Question 4(c)(ii) both test.
- If your school has a science set, run the three-card activity and the drawing-pin-on-a-rod activity before the mock. Pupils who have done them write far better answers to Questions 6(b) and 7(a).
