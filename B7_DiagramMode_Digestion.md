## ANSWER KEY AND MARKING SCHEME

**7.1  7.1 What is Food? Nutrients and Their Importance**

- **Q1** (4 marks) Maize supplies carbohydrate and fish supplies protein, so two of the six classes are covered and that is enough to fill the stomach and stop hunger. But the diagram shows six classes, and the other four are missing: fats and oil, vitamins, minerals and water. Not feeling hungry is not the test - a balanced diet means having all the classes in the right amounts, and each missing one has its own consequence. Without vitamin C the gums bleed and wounds heal slowly; without iron the blood cannot carry oxygen properly and the person is anaemic and tired; without calcium the bones stay weak; without enough fat the body cannot absorb some vitamins at all. So the person is fed but not nourished.
- **Q2** (2 marks) Any two, each with a correct source: carbohydrate - bread, rice, yam, maize; protein - fish, eggs, beans, meat; fats and oil - palm oil, butter, nuts; vitamins - fruit and vegetables; minerals - milk, salt, green leaves; water - clean drinking water.

**7.2  7.2 Why Humans Need to Eat**

- **Q3** (3 marks) The diagram shows that body mass depends on the BALANCE between energy in and energy out, not on eating fat as such. Cutting out fat does reduce the energy coming in, so on that point the pupil is right. But it is only half the answer, because the other side of the balance matters just as much: a person who eats only a little fat but takes in more carbohydrate than they use will still store the excess as body fat and gain weight. The body converts surplus energy from any class of food into stored fat. To lose weight the person must either take in less energy or use more, so that energy out exceeds energy in.

**7.3  7.3 The Digestive System: Parts of the Alimentary Canal**

- **Q4** (4 marks) The canal has to be long because its two jobs - digesting the food and absorbing the digested products - both need time and surface. A long tube gives the food time to pass slowly enough for the enzymes to finish their work, and it gives a large enough area of wall for the products to be absorbed through. If a large part of the small intestine were removed, both would suffer: the food would pass through too quickly for digestion to be completed, and there would be far less wall to absorb through. The undigested, unabsorbed food would be passed out as diarrhoea, and the person would lose weight and become malnourished even while eating normally.
- **Q5** (3 marks) Being part of the alimentary canal is about being part of the ROUTE the food takes, not about carrying out digestion. The canal is defined as the tube running from the mouth to the anus, and the oesophagus is a section of that tube. But the different sections have different jobs, and the oesophagus's job is only to carry the food - its muscles squeeze the food downwards in waves to the stomach. The diagram makes the point by listing 'nothing' in the chemical column for the oesophagus: it is a passage, not a place where food is broken down.

**7.4  7.4 What Happens to Food at Each Stage of Digestion**

- **Q6** (3 marks) Mechanical digestion breaks the food into smaller pieces without changing what it is - the teeth chew and grind it in the mouth, or the stomach wall churns it into a liquid, and it is still the same substance, only in smaller bits. Chemical digestion changes the food into a DIFFERENT substance, using enzymes and acid - for example saliva beginning to turn starch into sugar, or the stomach's enzymes attacking protein. One changes the size, the other changes the substance.
- **Q7** (4 marks) Chewing is mechanical digestion, and its effect is to break the food into many small pieces. Doing that greatly increases the SURFACE AREA of the food, and the enzymes that do the chemical digestion later can only attack the surface they can reach. So the more the food is broken up, the more of it the enzymes in the stomach and small intestine can work on at the same time, and the faster and more completely the chemical digestion goes. Chewing also mixes the food thoroughly with saliva and makes it easier to swallow. So although chewing does not itself finish any chemical digestion, it prepares the food so that the chemical digestion which follows can do its job properly.

**7.5  7.5 End Products of Digestion: Starch, Protein and Fats**

- **Q8** (3 marks) The diagram shows that glucose is the END PRODUCT of starch digestion - it is already small and soluble, so it is in exactly the form the body can use. Because it needs no further digesting, it can be put directly into the blood and the whole digestive system is bypassed. The patient is getting the product their gut would have produced but cannot, delivered by a route that does not depend on the gut working.
- **Q9** (4 marks) Although starch is built from glucose units, it is one enormous molecule, and it is far too big to pass through the wall of the small intestine and into the blood. It is also insoluble. The glucose units have to be separated from one another first, because only the individual small, soluble glucose molecules are able to cross the gut wall. So the body is not rejecting the glucose - it simply cannot reach it while it is locked up in the large molecule. Digestion is the unlocking, and until it happens the glucose in the starch is unavailable.

**7.6  7.6 Absorption of Digested Food in the Small Intestine**

- **Q10** (4 marks) Any two, each explained. There are thousands of villi, and the folds they make give an enormously large surface area, so a great deal of digested food can pass through the wall at the same moment. The wall of each villus is only one cell thick, so the food has a very short distance to travel to get into the blood, which makes diffusion quick. Each villus has a rich blood supply, and the blood carries the absorbed food away immediately, which keeps the concentration difference steep so that more food keeps diffusing in. A villus also has a lacteal, so fats are carried away too.
- **Q11** (3 marks) Fats pass into the LACTEAL, not into the blood capillary. The diagram shows the two vessels separately, with glucose and amino acids going to the capillary and fats to the lacteal. They take a different route because the products of fat digestion are handled differently by the body - they are taken up into the lymphatic system rather than being carried straight to the liver in the blood.

**7.7  7.7 Food Tests: Starch, Glucose, Protein, Fats and Oils**

- **Q12** (3 marks) Add a few drops of iodine solution to the food sample - or to a solution or paste made from it - in a test tube or on a white tile. Iodine starts brown. If starch is present the colour changes to blue-black; if starch is absent it stays brown. A control using pure water or a sample known to contain no starch is run alongside for comparison.
- **Q13** (3 marks) A green colour still shows that a reducing sugar such as glucose is present - the test has not failed, and the answer is not 'no sugar'. Benedict's is not simply a yes-or-no test: the colour it turns depends on roughly HOW MUCH reducing sugar there is. Brick-red means a lot; orange or green means a smaller amount. So the pupil's sample contains some glucose, but less of it than a sample giving brick-red. The pupil should report it as a positive result for a small amount of reducing sugar, and could compare the colour against known samples to estimate how much.

**PRACTICAL TASK**

- **Q14** (4 marks) Samples B and D contain starch - the iodine in those two tubes changed from brown to blue-black, which is the positive result, while A and C stayed brown. To check the result is reliable I would repeat the test: carry it out again on fresh portions of each of the four samples, and check that the same two give blue-black a second time. I would also make the comparison fair by using the same number of drops of iodine in every tube and the same amount of each sample. Results that can be repeated are the ones worth trusting.
- **Q15** (3 marks) The iodine test tests for STARCH and for nothing else. All the results show is that A and C contain no detectable starch - they say nothing at all about glucose, protein, fats, vitamins or minerals. Sample A or C could be almost pure protein and the iodine test would still stay brown, because iodine does not react with protein. To claim a sample contains no nutrients you would have to test it separately for each class, using Benedict's for glucose, Biuret for protein and ethanol for fats. A negative result in one test is only ever evidence about that one nutrient.

## EXAMINER NOTES

- Pitched at the compulsory diagram question of BECE Paper 2: 78% of the marks are at Apply level or above and 40% are Challenging.
- Q9 is the question that separates pupils who have the idea from those who have the words. 'Starch is made of glucose, so why can't the body use it?' The answer is size and solubility: the glucose is there, but locked inside a molecule too big to cross the gut wall. Pupils who say 'it is too big' without saying WHY that matters - it cannot get through into the blood - have not finished.
- Q7 on chewing is a surface-area question dressed up as a health-advice question. The chain is: chew, smaller pieces, larger surface area, more enzymes working at once, faster complete digestion. Four links, four marks. Most pupils stop at 'it helps you swallow'.
- Q3 on cutting out fat is the misconception I most want caught. Body mass is a BALANCE, and surplus carbohydrate is stored as fat just as surely as eaten fat is. Pupils who get this have understood 7.2 rather than memorised it.
- Q13 on the green Benedict's result is worth doing even if you have covered food tests before, because pupils treat these tests as pass or fail. They are not - the colour indicates roughly how much. A green result is a POSITIVE result for a small amount.
- Q15 is the same lesson at the level of experimental reasoning: a negative result in one test is evidence about one nutrient only. Pupils write 'contains no nutrients' straight after an iodine test. Insist they name the nutrient the test actually looked for.
- Fig. 8 is a real experiment that any school can run with iodine and four samples - floor, gari, sugar solution and cooked rice work well and give two positives and two negatives. Get pupils to predict first, then test; the predictions are where the thinking shows.
