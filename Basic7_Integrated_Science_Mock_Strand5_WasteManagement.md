### Question 4 (8 marks)

**(a)** [6 marks]

### Question 5 (9 marks)

**(a)** [3 marks]

- (i) the top layer - a covering of soil, dry grass or dry leaves
- (ii) the middle layer - the organic waste being composted, such as food remains and green leaves
- (iii) the bottom layer - coarse dry material such as stalks, husks and dry grass
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for all THREE correct labels (3 marks). Accept any answer that correctly identifies the MIDDLE layer as the waste being composted and the TOP layer as a covering; if a pupil reverses the top and bottom layers but gets the middle right, award 2 marks. Do NOT accept 'compost' for any of the three - compost is what the pit PRODUCES.*

**(b)** [3 marks]

- Bacteria and fungi in the soil feed on the waste and break it down.
- The waste is changed into simple substances such as water, carbon dioxide and humus.
- The process is called decomposition, and it needs moisture, warmth and air to go well.
- The heap is turned over from time to time to let air in and to mix the materials.
- After some weeks the waste becomes a dark, crumbly material called compost.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). The two ideas to look for are MICRO-ORGANISMS doing the breaking down and the conditions (moist, warm, air) that let them work.*

**(c)** [3 marks]

- Plastic is made of long chains of molecules that bacteria and fungi cannot digest.
- The micro-organisms that rot food remains have no way of breaking plastic down.
- So plastic stays in the soil for a very long time instead of rotting away.
- Food remains are made of the same kind of material as living things, so micro-organisms can feed on them easily.
- Plastic may break into small pieces, but it does not rot into simple substances.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). The key idea is that there are no micro-organisms that can digest plastic. Do NOT accept 'plastic is too strong' or 'plastic is too hard'.*

### EXAMINER NOTES

- Question 5(a) is the item pupils are most likely to argue about, because a compost pit can be built in more than one order. The note accepts the standard answer - covering on top, waste in the middle, coarse material at the bottom - but still awards 2 marks to a pupil who reverses the top and bottom layers provided they identify the middle layer as the waste. The one answer that earns nothing is 'compost' for any of the three labels: compost is what the pit produces, not a layer in it.
- Question 7(a) is the design task and it is the one that carries the indicator's real demand - the curriculum asks pupils to USE what they have researched to carry out a project. The note caps the answer at 3 of 4 if it does no more than say the class will sweep the compound, and it looks for at least one point about planning and one about what happens to the waste afterwards. Safety earns a mark, so tell them to mention gloves and boots.
- Question 4(a) is marked twice over: one mark for the meaning of each of the three terms and one for its example. Pupils who know reduce, reuse and recycle perfectly but write no examples lose half the marks. Worth drilling before the mock.
- Questions 2(a) and 1(b) both turn on the biodegradable line, and the two that catch pupils out are paper and glass. Paper rots, so it is biodegradable; glass does not, so it is not. Several pupils assume that anything recycled must be non-biodegradable.
- Topic weighting is 28 marks for 16.1, 26 for 16.2, 27 for 16.3 and 19 for 16.4. If you spent longer on the community project than on types of waste, move marks towards Question 7.
- Two cheap practicals that raise marks sharply: (1) bury a banana peel, a piece of paper, a polythene bag and a nail in a box of soil, water it, and dig them up after three weeks; and (2) start a class compost pit behind the science block and use it all term. The first teaches Questions 1, 2 and 5, the second teaches Questions 3, 5 and 7.
- Question 7 is worth doing for real rather than only on paper. One afternoon of cleaning the school compound, followed by a short report presented to the class, satisfies the indicator directly and gives every pupil something concrete to write about in the mock.
