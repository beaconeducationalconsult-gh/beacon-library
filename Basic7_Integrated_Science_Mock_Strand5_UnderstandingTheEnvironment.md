### Question 3 (9 marks)

**(a)** [3 marks]

- Adaptation is any feature or behaviour of a living thing that helps it to survive in the place where it lives.
- For example, a grasscutter has strong front teeth for cutting tough roots, and it lives in a burrow to escape the heat of the day.
  - *Marking note: Up to 3 marks: up to 2 for a correct definition that says the feature or behaviour HELPS the living thing to SURVIVE WHERE IT LIVES, and 1 for a correct example. A definition that says only 'it is when an animal changes to fit in' earns 1 at most, because an adaptation is a feature the animal already has, not one it chooses.*

**(b)** [4 marks]

- Long legs, so that the animal can run fast across open ground where there is nowhere to hide.
- A coat the same colour as the dry grass, so that enemies cannot see it easily.
- Living in a burrow, which is cooler than the open ground and hides the animal from enemies.
- Large ears or thin skin, which let the body give off heat and keep cool.
- The ability to go for a long time without drinking.
  - *Marking note: Up to 4 marks: up to 2 for each of TWO characteristics, 1 for naming it and 1 for explaining HOW it helps. Give 0 for a characteristic that is named but not explained. Do NOT accept a bare list of animals.*

**(c)** [2 marks]

- An animal on a mountain would need thicker covering against the cold and feet that grip rock, while an animal on a plain needs to run fast and to keep cool.
- The reason is that the mountain is colder, wetter and steeper, while the plain is hot, dry and open.
  - *Marking note: Up to 2 marks: 1 for a sensible difference and 1 for a correct reason that refers to the conditions in each landform. A pupil who names a difference but cannot say why it matters earns 1. Do NOT accept 'they are different because they live in different places'.*

### Question 4 (8 marks)

**(a)** [4 marks]

- It may shed its leaves in the dry season, so that no water is lost from them while there is none in the soil.
- It may have small, tough or waxy leaves, which lose water very slowly.
- It may store water in a swollen trunk, a thick stem or an underground bulb.
- It may send a very long root down to reach water far below the surface.
- It may die back to a bulb or a swollen root underground and grow again when the rains come.
- Its seeds may lie in the soil and wait until there has been enough rain.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any FOUR different ways (4 marks). Give no more than 1 mark in total for ways that are only named and not explained. Do NOT accept 'it stores water' with nothing said about where, and do NOT accept the same way written twice in different words.*

**(b)** [4 marks]

- Water is plentiful in the valley and scarce on the plateau, so valley plants can afford broad, thin leaves, while a plant with broad leaves on a dry plateau would lose water faster than its roots could replace it.
- The valley is sheltered and its soil is usually deeper and richer, so plants there can grow larger, while the plateau is open to wind and strong sun.
  - *Marking note: Up to 4 marks: up to 2 for each of TWO reasons, 1 for the difference in conditions and 1 for explaining how that produces the difference in the plants. A pupil who says only 'the valley has more water' earns 1. Do NOT accept 'valley plants are just bigger'.*

### Question 5 (9 marks)

**(a)** [3 marks]

- Commensalism is an association between two living things in which one benefits and the other is neither helped nor harmed.
- For example, a small bird builds its nest in a tree. The bird gets a safe place and the tree is not affected.
  - *Marking note: Up to 3 marks: up to 2 for a correct definition that says ONE BENEFITS and the OTHER IS NOT AFFECTED, and 1 for a correct example. A definition that says 'both benefit' earns 0, because that is mutualism. Do NOT accept an example in which the second living thing is harmed.*

**(b)** [4 marks]

- Mutualism - for example, bees and flowering plants. The bee gets nectar for food and the plant gets its pollen carried to another flower so that it can make seeds. Both benefit.
- Parasitism - for example, a tick on a goat, or mistletoe on a tree. The tick gets blood and the goat loses blood and may grow weak or fall sick, so the parasite benefits and the host is harmed.
  - *Marking note: Up to 4 marks: up to 2 for each association, 1 for a correct named example and 1 for correctly saying who benefits and who is harmed. Give 0 for a correct example with no statement of the effect. Do NOT accept an example of commensalism under either heading.*

**(c)** [2 marks]

- Parasitism, because the parasite takes food or blood from its host, so the host grows slowly, becomes weak and may die before it can reproduce.
- Accept also: a heavy growth of mistletoe or a strangler fig can kill the trees, which then removes the shelter and the food that other living things depend on.
  - *Marking note: Up to 2 marks: 1 for naming parasitism and 1 for explaining that the host is weakened or killed. A pupil who names parasitism with no reason earns 1. Accept commensalism or mutualism only if it is argued soundly, and then cap the answer at 1.*

### Question 6 (8 marks)

**(a)** [3 marks]

- (i) The egrets and the cattle - commensalism. The egrets get the insects the cattle disturb, and the cattle are not affected.
- (ii) The ticks and the cattle - parasitism. The ticks get blood and the cattle are harmed.
- (iii) The fig and the other tree - parasitism. The fig gets support and light, and the other tree is killed.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for the three correct associations (3 marks). Accept 'mutualism' for the egrets only if the pupil explains that the cattle are also freed of insects. Give 0 if the pupil names the right association but pairs it with the wrong living things.*

**(b)** [3 marks]

- The ticks are the most harmful, because they feed on the cattle's blood, make them weak and can give them disease, so the farmer loses milk, meat and work from the animals.
- Accept also the fig, if it is argued well: it kills a tree that gives shade, fruit or protection to the soil on the farm.
  - *Marking note: Up to 3 marks: 1 for a clear choice, 1 for explaining the harm and 1 for saying what the farmer loses. A pupil who chooses but gives no reason earns 1 only. Do NOT accept 'all of them are equally harmful' unless it is argued in detail.*

**(c)** [2 marks]

- Spray or dip the cattle to kill the ticks, and do it again so that the eggs which hatch later are killed too.
- Clear the bushes where the ticks wait, or move the cattle to another pasture for a time to break the cycle.
- Keep the cattle in a clean, dry kraal at night and check them often for ticks.
- Cut down the fig before it kills the tree, and plant another tree in its place.
  - *Marking note: Up to 2 marks: 1 mark each for any TWO sensible actions, each with what it does. Give no more than 1 mark for a bare list with no explanation. Do NOT accept 'kill the cattle'.*

### Question 7 (9 marks)

**(a)** [2 marks]

- The name of the landform and where in Ghana it is found, for example the Kwahu Plateau in the Eastern Region.
- Pictures or drawings of the plants and animals found there, each one named.
- A note on the conditions there - whether it is wet or dry, hot or cool.
- A note on any adaptation that helps a plant or an animal to survive there.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any TWO sensible contents (2 marks). Do NOT accept contents that have nothing to do with landforms, such as 'a list of the class' or 'the date it was made'.*

**(b)** [4 marks]

- Accept any sensible design. For example: the album will have ten pages - a title page, two pages for each of the four landforms (plateau, plain, mountain and valley), and a page at the back comparing them. Each landform gets a left-hand page with a drawing of its shape and a short note on its soil, water and weather, and a right-hand page with pictures of three plants and three animals found there, each named underneath. A contents page at the front lists the four landforms. Every page is titled with the landform and the region of Ghana where it is found.
  - *Marking note: Up to 4 marks: 1 for saying how many pages there will be or how the album is divided, 1 for what goes on each page, 1 for how it is organised or ordered, and 1 for naming the landforms it covers. Give no more than 2 for 'we will put pictures in it' with no structure. Do NOT accept an album that covers only one landform.*

**(c)** [3 marks]

- Find the information from the class textbook, the school library, the internet or the district agriculture office, and check each plant and animal against a second source or a person who knows, such as the science teacher or an elder who farms there.
- Draw the plants and animals from life during a field visit, or print pictures and check the name under each one before using it.
- Take the finished album to the science teacher to check, and correct anything that is wrong before making the final copy.
  - *Marking note: Up to 3 marks: 1 for where or how the information is collected, 1 for HOW IT IS CHECKED and 1 for a second check or a named source or person. An answer that says only 'we will look on the internet' earns 1 at most, and 0 if nothing is said about checking.*

### EXAMINER NOTES

- Question 5 is where most marks will be lost. Pupils mix up the three associations, and the commonest error is writing 'both benefit' for commensalism, which earns 0. Drill the three as a fixed pattern: mutualism - both gain; parasitism - one gains, one is harmed; commensalism - one gains, the other is not affected at all. Insist they state all three words in every answer.
- Question 3(a) and Question 5(b) both turn on definitions, and pupils lose marks for saying 'adaptation is when an animal changes to fit in'. An adaptation is a feature or behaviour the living thing already has. Correct this before the examination, not after.
- Question 2 is the completion table. Blank (iii) is the hard one - pupils will put baobab or oil palm on the mountain, and the note does not accept them. Moss, fern and lichen are the answers to teach. Blank (i) is the easy one, so a pupil who scores 2 of 8 has probably only guessed at the savanna.
- Question 4(a) asks for FOUR explained ways, and pupils tend to write one way four times. The note allows no more than 1 mark in total for ways that are named but not explained. Teach them four clearly different mechanisms: shedding leaves, small or waxy leaves, storing water, and a deep root.
- Question 7(b) is the Create task. Pupils find it easy to say 'we will put pictures in it' and hard to specify pages, organisation and contents, which is why that answer is capped at 2 of 4. Practise one album plan on paper in class before the examination and the marks rise sharply.
- Topic weighting is 25 marks each for 20.2 and 20.5, 17 each for 20.1 and 20.3, and 16 for 20.4. The two heavier topics carry two Section B questions each. If your class found associations hard, spend the extra time on Question 5 and Question 6.
- Three cheap activities that raise marks: (1) take the class to the nearest slope, flat ground and stream and have them list what lives in each, which teaches Questions 1, 2 and 3; (2) collect a waxy leaf, a hairy leaf and a broad thin leaf and pass them round, which teaches Question 4; and (3) have each group find one real example of each association on the school compound, which teaches Questions 5 and 6.
