### Question 1 (9 marks)

**(a)** [3 marks]

- A viral disease is an illness caused by a virus.
- It is an infectious disease, so it can spread from one person to another.
- Examples are COVID-19, Ebola, H1N1 influenza, measles and chicken pox.
- The virus enters the body and multiplies inside the body's living cells.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). The key idea is that the cause is a VIRUS. Do NOT accept 'a disease caused by bacteria' or 'a disease caused by a lack of food'.*

**(b)** [3 marks]

- A virus is much smaller than a bacterium; it cannot be seen with an ordinary school microscope.
- A virus can only multiply INSIDE a living cell, while many bacteria can multiply outside cells.
- Antibiotics kill bacteria but have no effect on viruses.
- Bacteria are living cells; a virus is not a complete cell.
- Viral diseases can often be prevented by vaccination; fewer bacterial diseases are.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct differences (3 marks). Accept any genuine difference. Do NOT accept 'viruses are bad and bacteria are good' or any answer that treats all bacteria as harmless.*

**(c)** [3 marks]

- Antibiotics are medicines that kill BACTERIA or stop them from multiplying.
- A virus is not a bacterium and does not have the parts that antibiotics attack.
- Because a virus multiplies inside the body's own cells, a medicine that attacked it would also harm the body's cells.
- So antibiotics cannot destroy the virus and will not cure the illness.
- Taking antibiotics when they are not needed can also cause harm later, because bacteria become resistant to them.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). The key idea is that antibiotics act on bacteria and not on viruses. Award at most 1 mark for an answer that only says 'they do not work'.*

- Leaving the person at home means carers and family members are infected, and they carry it to others.
- Taking the person to a health facility protects the family and helps the health workers stop the outbreak.
- There is no cure that the family can give at home, so delay costs lives.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). The key ideas are that the person is highly infectious, that they need skilled care, and that leaving them in the community spreads the disease.*

### Question 6 (8 marks)

**(a)** [3 marks]

- H1N1 is a viral disease; it is a kind of influenza or flu that affects people.
- It was first called swine flu because the virus is related to flu viruses found in pigs.
- Symptoms include fever and chills.
- A cough and a sore throat.
- A running nose or a blocked nose.
- Body aches, headache and tiredness.
- Some people vomit or have diarrhoea.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark for explaining that H1N1 is a viral flu and 1 mark each for any TWO correct symptoms (3 marks). Award at most 2 marks if the explanation is missing.*

**(b)** [3 marks]

- SIMILAR: both are caused by a virus and both can be declared a pandemic.
- SIMILAR: both spread through droplets from coughing and sneezing.
- SIMILAR: both cause fever, cough and tiredness, and both can make breathing difficult.
- SIMILAR: both are prevented by handwashing, masks, distance and avoiding crowds.
- DIFFERENT: Ebola spreads through body fluids rather than mainly through the air, and it causes bleeding.
- DIFFERENT: Ebola kills a far larger share of the people it infects.
- DIFFERENT: H1N1 is a flu that most people recover from, while Ebola is much more severe.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark for each of TWO correct similarities and 1 mark for one correct difference (3 marks). Accept comparisons with either COVID-19 or Ebola, provided the point is accurate.*

**(c)** [2 marks]

- Wash the hands often with soap and water.
- Cover the mouth and nose when coughing or sneezing.
- Keep away from crowds and keep a distance from anyone who is coughing.
- Get the seasonal flu vaccine where it is available.
- Stay at home and rest when feeling unwell, and do not go to school or work.
- Clean surfaces that are touched often and keep rooms well aired.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any TWO correct ways (2 marks). Accept any sensible preventive measure.*

**(a)** [3 marks]

### EXAMINER NOTES

- Question 1(c) is the item that separates understanding from rote learning. Pupils who have only memorised a list will say 'antibiotics do not work'. The marks are on why: antibiotics attack bacteria, a virus has none of the parts they attack, and a virus hides inside the body's own cells. Worth teaching the reason, not just the rule.
- Questions 5(b) and 3(b) sit next to each other on purpose, because pupils merge the two routes. COVID-19 and H1N1 travel in droplets from the breath; Ebola travels in body fluids and NOT through the air over a distance. Both notes cap the answer at 2 if the route is given for the wrong disease, so drill the pair together.
- Question 7(b) is the design task, and it is marked on two halves: what the poster SHOWS or SAYS, and how it is made clear to someone who cannot read. Many pupils will produce a good slogan and forget the second half. The note caps them at 3 of 4 for that.
- Question 4(b) is deliberately open. Pupils who lived through the school closures have real views about it, and the note credits either side provided the reasons support the position. Do not mark down a pupil who argues that closing schools was wrong, if the reasons are good.
- Question 6(b) asks for two similarities and one difference, and pupils may compare H1N1 with either COVID-19 or Ebola. Both are accepted. The one answer to watch for is a 'difference' that is really a similarity written twice.
- Topic weighting is 26 marks for 17.4, 28 for 17.5, 24 for 17.6 and 22 for 17.7. If you spent longer on community roles than on the nature of viruses, move marks towards Question 7.
- Two cheap activities that raise marks sharply: (1) put glitter or flour on one pupil's hands and let the class see how far it travels by touch in five minutes, and (2) have the class design a real poster and then read it to a pupil from a lower class to test whether the message lands. The first teaches Question 2, the second teaches Question 7.
- The companion paper covers topics 17.1 to 17.3 - food nutrients, deficiency diseases, and night blindness, rickets, scurvy, kwashiorkor and anaemia - under content standard B7/JHS1.5.2.1. Use the two papers together for a full end-of-term examination on this sub-strand, or separately as two class tests.
