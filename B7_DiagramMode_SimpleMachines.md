## ANSWER KEY AND MARKING SCHEME

**14.9  14.9 Simple Machines and Their Types**

- **Q1** (4 marks) The work is identical: lifting straight up is 500 N x 1 m = 500 J, and pushing up the slope is 125 N x 4 m = 500 J. What the slope changes is not the amount of work but the SIZE OF THE FORCE you must supply at any one moment. Lifting straight up demands a single pull of 500 N, which is beyond one person. The slope lets the same job be done with a pull of only 125 N - a force one person can manage - but you have to keep pushing over 4 m instead of 1 m. So 'easier' means 'with a smaller effort', not 'with less work'. The machine changes the force and charges you in distance; it cannot reduce the total work.
- **Q2** (3 marks) A machine is a device that makes work easier by changing the size or the direction of a force. No machine can give out more work than is put into it - it cannot produce more energy than it receives, and some of what goes in is always lost as heat.

**14.10  14.10 Types and Functions of Levers**

- **Q3** (4 marks) (a) A lever. It makes the force bigger: a small pull on the handle produces a large pull on the cap. (b) A pulley. It changes the direction: you pull down on the rope and the flag travels up. The effort is about the same size as the load, which is the price of that convenience. (c) A wedge. It makes the force bigger and turns it sideways: a downward blow becomes a large splitting force pushing the wood apart. (d) An inclined plane. It makes the force smaller for the same job: pushing the load up the ramp needs far less force than lifting it straight onto the truck, but over a longer distance.
- **Q4** (3 marks) Any three of: lever, pulley, inclined plane, wheel and axle, screw, wedge, gears, hydraulic press.
- **Q5** (3 marks) A spanner is a lever, and the turning effect it produces depends on both the force you apply and how far from the pivot - the nut - it acts. With a long spanner, the same push from your hand acts at a greater distance from the nut, so it gives a bigger turning effect and the nut gives way. With a short spanner you would have to push much harder to get the same turning effect, and beyond a point you simply cannot.
- **Q6** (3 marks) If you could unwrap the thread of a screw and lay it out flat, it would be one long sloping line - an inclined plane wrapped round a cylinder. Driving the screw in is like pushing a load up that slope: the further you push along the slope for each centimetre gained, the less force you need. Closely spaced threads mean many turns for every centimetre the screw advances, so the slope is shallower and the same push gives a larger inward force. The cost is that you must turn the screw many more times to drive it the same depth.

**14.11  14.11 Work Input, Work Output and Efficiency**

- **Q7** (4 marks) The velocity ratio is fixed by the geometry of the machine - how much further the effort travels than the load does - and nothing can alter it. The mechanical advantage is the ratio of the load actually lifted to the effort actually needed. In a perfect machine the two would be equal and the efficiency would be 100%. But in a real machine part of your effort goes into overcoming friction between the moving parts, and part into lifting the machine's own moving parts, neither of which benefits the load. So you have to pull harder than the geometry alone demands, the mechanical advantage comes out below the velocity ratio, and the difference is work converted into heat. That gap is precisely what efficiency measures, as M.A. over V.R.
- **Q8** (4 marks) Mechanical advantage = load / effort = 200 N / 50 N = 4. Velocity ratio = 5. Efficiency = M.A. / V.R. x 100% = 4 / 5 x 100% = 80%. So 80% of the work put in comes out as useful work on the load. The missing 20% has been converted into heat by friction between the moving parts of the machine, and into raising the machine's own moving parts. The energy has not been destroyed - it has simply ended up somewhere useless, warming the machine and the air around it. That is why no machine can ever reach 100% efficiency.
- **Q9** (3 marks) Any two, each explained. Oil or grease the axle so the sheave turns more freely, which cuts the friction that turns useful work into heat. Fit ball bearings at the axle for the same reason. Use lighter, more flexible rope, so less of the effort is spent lifting and bending the rope itself rather than the load. Lift a heavier load: the effort wasted on friction and on the moving parts stays roughly the same, so as a fraction of a bigger useful output it shrinks, and the efficiency rises.
- **Q10** (3 marks) The ratio of the distance moved by the effort to the distance moved by the load, measured over the same time. It says how much further your hand has to travel than the load does. Unlike the mechanical advantage it is fixed by the shape and arrangement of the machine and is not affected by friction.

**PRACTICAL TASK**

- **Q11** (4 marks) The effort needed depends entirely on how the pivot divides the lever. In set-up 1 the effort arm is 80 cm and the load arm 20 cm - four times as long - and the 10 N load needs only 2.6 N, so the lever is multiplying the effort about fourfold. In set-up 2 the two arms are equal at 50 cm each; there is no multiplication at all and the effort is 10.4 N, essentially the weight of the load plus a little extra for friction. In set-up 3 the arms are reversed, 20 cm and 80 cm, and the effort jumps to 42 N - now the lever works against you and you push roughly four times harder than the load weighs. So the longer the effort arm is compared with the load arm, the less effort is needed, and the ratio of the two arms is the velocity ratio.
- **Q12** (4 marks) Velocity ratio = effort arm / load arm = 80 cm / 20 cm = 4.0. Mechanical advantage = load / effort = 10 N / 2.6 N = 3.8 (3.85 to 3 s.f.). Efficiency = M.A. / V.R. x 100% = 3.8 / 4.0 x 100% = 95%. An efficiency of about 95% says that nearly all the work put in comes out as useful work lifting the load, and only about 5% is lost. The loss is friction where the lever turns on the pivot, and it is small because a lever has only one rubbing surface. That is why the lever is among the most efficient of the simple machines. Notice too that the efficiency stays near 95% in all three set-ups - the friction is much the same whatever the geometry, so changing the pivot position changes the effort needed but not how much is wasted.
- **Q13** (4 marks) He should place the pivot as close to the stone as he can get it, leaving the longest possible effort arm and the shortest possible load arm. That gives the largest velocity ratio, so the force-multiplying effect is at its greatest and the effort needed is at its smallest. The disadvantage is distance: because the work is the same either way, gaining force costs movement, so he must now push his end of the lever through a much greater distance to raise the stone by a little. He also needs a longer, stiffer lever, strong enough not to bend, and he needs room to swing it.
- **Q14** (2 marks) The distance from the pivot to the point where the effort is applied.
- **Q15** (2 marks) The force that the machine has to overcome - here, the weight of the object being lifted, which is the 10 N weight hanging from the lever.

## EXAMINER NOTES

- Pitched at the compulsory diagram question of BECE Paper 2: 74% of the marks are at Apply level or above and 40% are Challenging.
- Q1 is the question the whole strand rests on, and it is worth doing slowly at the board. Pupils come in believing a machine 'saves work'. It does not: 500 J either way. What it does is let you use a smaller force, and it charges you for that in distance. Get this right and mechanical advantage, velocity ratio and efficiency all follow from it; get it wrong and the rest of 14.11 is arithmetic without meaning.
- Q7 and Q8 are a pair. Q7 asks why M.A. is always below V.R.; Q8 makes them calculate it. The sentence to listen for is 'friction takes its cut' - the difference between the two ratios is work turned into heat, not work that vanished.
- Q6 on the screw as a spiral inclined plane is the one pupils enjoy, and it is worth drawing the unwrapped thread on the board. It also explains something they have all noticed: fine threads are easier to drive but take far longer to go in.
- Q12 is the calculation question, and the interesting part is the last sentence. The efficiency stays near 95% across all three set-ups because the friction at the pivot does not care where the pivot is. Pupils expect efficiency to vary with the geometry; it does not, and saying why is worth more than the arithmetic.
- For the lever experiment you need a metre rule or a wooden lath, a triangular or round pivot, a 10 N weight and a spring balance. Drill or notch the rule at 20, 50 and 80 cm so the pivot cannot roll. The awkward measurement is the effort in set-up 3 - 42 N will overload a 10 N balance, so use a 50 N balance for that one, or predict the value first and let pupils discover the problem themselves.
- If balances are short, run the three set-ups as a demonstration with pupils recording the readings, then let groups repeat just one set-up each.
