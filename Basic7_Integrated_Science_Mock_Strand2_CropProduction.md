### Question 1 (8 marks)

**(a)** [2 marks]

- A plant nutrient is any substance that a plant takes in and uses for its growth and development.
- It is a substance that feeds the plant and helps it to grow, produce leaves, flowers, fruits and seeds.
- It is the 'food' of the plant, taken mainly from the soil, air and water.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any TWO correct points (2 marks). Award 1 mark for 'something the plant needs to grow' if it is not developed further. Do NOT accept 'fertilizer' on its own - a fertilizer is a source of nutrients, not the nutrient itself.*

**(b)** [3 marks]

- To grow tall and produce more leaves and stems.
- To produce a good root system.
- To produce flowers, fruits and seeds (a good yield).
- To stay healthy and resist disease and drought.
- To give a dark green leaf colour (nitrogen builds the green colouring).
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). Accept 'to grow well' once. Do NOT award three marks for the same point worded three times.*

**(c)** [3 marks]

- Every harvest carries nutrients away from the land in the grain, leaves and stalks that are removed.
- Cropping year after year therefore takes more nutrients out of the soil than are put back.
- Rain also washes some nutrients deeper into the soil or off the land altogether.
- So the store of nutrients in the soil becomes smaller and the crop becomes weak and the yield falls, unless the farmer replaces them.
- Adding fertilizer or manure puts back what the crop has taken out.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). The key idea is that the harvest removes nutrients, so they must be replaced. Award 1 mark for 'so that the soil does not become poor'.*

### Question 4 (9 marks)

**(a)** [3 marks]

- It is dry.
- It is in the form of granules, crystals or powder.
- The particles are small and fairly uniform in size.
- It is often coloured (white, grey, pink or blue).
- It is packed and sold in labelled bags.
- It has little or no smell of decay.
- It dissolves readily in water.
- It is heavy for its size.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct characteristics (3 marks). Accept 'it is bagged' or 'it looks like salt or sugar'.*

**(b)** [3 marks]

- It is dark brown or black.
- It is crumbly or powdery when it is well rotted.
- It is moist or damp.
- It smells of decay (like rotting leaves or manure).
- It is bulky: a little of it takes up a lot of space for its mass.
- It contains visible pieces of straw, husks, leaves or fibre.
- The particles are of many different sizes.
- It is light for its size.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct characteristics (3 marks). Accept 'it looks like dark soil'.*

**(c)** [3 marks]

- Because NPK is dry and flows freely, it can be measured out with a tin or a cup and placed in a ring or band beside each plant.
- Because it dissolves quickly, it must be covered with soil and the soil kept moist, otherwise rain washes it away before the roots reach it.
- Because it is concentrated, a small measured amount is enough, and too much in one place will burn the plant.
- Because compost is bulky and coarse, it is spread over the whole bed and mixed into the topsoil rather than measured into small rings.
- Because compost is coarse and holds water, it can also be spread on the surface as a mulch.
- A dusty powdery fertilizer must not be applied on a windy day, because the wind will blow it away.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). The answer must say something about BOTH the inorganic form and the organic form to earn full marks; award at most 2 marks if only one type is dealt with.*

### Question 5 (9 marks)

**(a)** [3 marks]

- Wait until the soil is moist, for example just after rain, or water the plot first.
- Measure out the amount needed for the number of plants.
- Place it in a ring or a band about 5-10 cm from the base of each maize plant.
- Cover it with a thin layer of soil so that the rain does not wash it away.
- Apply it in two or three smaller doses (split application) during the growing season instead of all at once.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). The key ideas are: moist soil, measured amount, placed beside the plant, and covered with soil.*

**(b)** [3 marks]

- Spread the compost evenly over the whole bed or plot.
- Work it lightly into the topsoil with a hoe or a rake.
- Do this before planting, or round the base of established plants without touching the stem.
- Water the plot afterwards so that the nutrients begin to move into the soil.
- Use a generous layer, because compost is bulky and contains less nutrient per kilogram.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). Accept spreading it as a mulch round the plants.*

**(c)** [3 marks]

- Fertilizer placed against the stem can draw water out of the young tissues and burn or kill the plant.
- Placing it a few centimetres away puts the nutrient where the feeding roots are, which spread sideways from the plant.
- Covering it with soil stops it from being washed away by rain or blown away by the wind.
- Covering also keeps the nutrient from being lost to the air before the roots can take it up.
- A ring is better than a heap, because the nutrient is spread where the roots can reach it.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). One mark must come from an explanation of the burning/scorching risk; award at most 2 marks if the answer only deals with the roots.*

### Question 6 (10 marks)

**(a)** [3 marks]

- A hoe or a hand fork
- A rake
- A bucket or a basin to carry the compost
- A measuring tin, cup or small bucket
- A watering can with water
- String and pegs to mark out the bed
- Gloves
- A nose mask
- Boots or old shoes
- A notebook and pencil for recording
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct tools or materials (3 marks). Accept any sensible item a pupil would really use in a school garden.*

**(b)** [3 marks]

- Clear the bed of weeds and loosen the topsoil.
- Mark out the bed and the planting positions with string and pegs.
- Measure out the compost with a tin or a bucket so that every part of the bed gets the same amount.
- Spread the compost evenly over the surface of the bed.
- Work it lightly into the topsoil with a hoe or a rake, without damaging the roots of nearby plants.
- Plant or water in the seedlings and water the bed well afterwards.
- Record the date, the amount used and what was observed.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct steps in a sensible order (3 marks). Award method marks: a sensible sequence that would work in a real school garden earns credit even if the wording is simple.*

**(c)** [4 marks]

- A column for the date of each observation.
- A column naming the bed or plot (e.g. Bed A with compost, Bed B without compost).
- A column recording what was applied and how much.
- A column for plant height, measured with a ruler or a stick at regular intervals.
- A column for the number of leaves, or for leaf colour and general vigour.
- A column for the yield: the number or the mass of the garden eggs harvested.
- A column for any other observation, such as pests, wilting or rainfall.
- A second bed with no compost, so that the class has something to compare with.
- Space to write the pupil's name or group and any comments.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any FOUR sensible features of the record sheet (4 marks). This is a design task: award method marks generously for any feature that would help the class decide whether the compost made a difference. A comparison bed, a measurement taken over time, and a record of what was applied are the three features to look for first.*

### Question 7 (5 marks)

**(a)** [3 marks]

- Mark out two (or more) beds of the same size, in the same place and with the same soil.
- Plant the same crop, of the same variety, at the same spacing, on the same day.
- Give every bed the same water, the same weeding and the same care.
- Change only ONE thing: the type of nutrient source (compost in one bed, NPK in the other).
- Keep one bed with no nutrient at all as a control, so the class can see the difference.
- Work out the amount of each nutrient source beforehand by measuring, and write it down.
- Repeat the comparison, or use more than one bed of each type, so that one unlucky bed does not spoil the result.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any THREE correct points (3 marks). The key idea is a FAIR TEST: only the type of nutrient is changed and everything else is kept the same. Award at least 1 mark if the pupil states this principle even if the practical detail is thin.*

**(b)** [2 marks]

- The height of the plants in each bed, measured at the same time.
- The colour of the leaves (dark green or pale yellow-green).
- The number of leaves, or how big the stems are.
- How quickly the plants flower or start to fruit.
- The number or the mass of the crop harvested from each bed.
- How much water each bed needed.
  - *Marking note: 1 mark each for any TWO sensible observations (2 marks). Accept any observation that can be compared between the beds. Do NOT award a mark for 'which one is better' without saying what was observed.*

- Question 3(a) is the 'create a table' exemplar (B7/JHS1.2.3.1.1, Ex. 1) turned into a completion task. The six blanks are numbered (i) to (vi) so marking is quick and fair: 1 mark each, and the blank number must be matched to the answer.
- Question 7 is the volume-comparison exemplar (B7/JHS1.2.3.1.1, Ex. 2) set up as a fair test. The thing to look for is the principle that only the type of nutrient changes - same bed size, same crop, same spacing, same water and a control bed.
- Question 6 is the practical-application exemplar (B7/JHS1.2.3.1.2, Ex. 3). If your school has a garden, run Questions 6 and 7 as real activities before the mock, and accept what pupils actually did as evidence in the answers.
- Topic 5.6 carries 21 of 100 marks (21%), the highest weighting, because the indicator ends in practical application. If you want a flatter spread, move marks from Questions 6 and 7 to Questions 1 and 2.
- A6 and A20 deliberately avoid any calculation. The figures on an NPK bag (A24) are tested as labels to read, not as numbers to work with - that is the B7 limit.
- If you supply real samples of compost, manure and NPK during revision, pupils handle A21 to A27 far better, since those items are about what the material looks and feels like.
- Safety note for any practical follow-up: gloves, hand washing, no eating in the garden, and no handling of fertilizer dust without supervision. Do not let pupils taste or smell fertilizers.
